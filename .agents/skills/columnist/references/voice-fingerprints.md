# Voice Fingerprints

Detailed *compositional* breakdowns of how each named writer actually builds prose at the line level. Read this in Phase 3 (Pick the voice) when committing to a voice or blend.

These describe *craft moves at the sentence level* — opening sentence patterns, signature compositional moves, sentence shapes, diction register and where it shifts, punctuation as voice, lexical tells. The earlier version of this file was too structural (where the lede sits, how the argument unfolds). This version focuses on what a writer's prose actually *does* line by line.

The opinion-essay tradition (Graham through Petersen) and the narrative-journalism / personal-memoir / academic-management registers are deliberately separated below — voices generally blend within a register but not across.

---

# Part I — The opinion-essay tradition

## Paul Graham

**Signature compositional move.** A short Anglo-Saxon sentence stating a position, followed by a longer sentence that unspools the reasoning, followed by another short sentence that lands a consequence. Short–long–short, with the longest sentence doing the actual thinking.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A flat declarative, 6–14 words, monosyllabic-leaning. No throat-clearing, no "in this essay I will." The reader knows the thesis by the end of sentence one.

**Diction.** Anglo-Saxon almost exclusively. *Make* not *produce*, *use* not *leverage*, *kids* not *children*, *get* not *obtain*, *try* not *attempt*. When a technical term is unavoidable, defined in the same sentence. Avoids adverbs.

**Sentence shapes.** Wildly varied within paragraphs. A four-word sentence followed by a 35-word one that contains the actual reasoning followed by another short one. The variation isn't decorative — the long sentences earn their length by doing logical work; the short ones land punches.

**Punctuation as voice.** Commas. Periods. Em-dashes used rarely, for actual interruption rather than as fancy commas. **Numbered footnotes** for tangents that would derail the main line — this is one of Graham's strongest signatures. [1] [2] [3].

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- *"I think"* before claims, to mark hypothesis vs. fact.
- *"And yet"* to signal a logical pivot.
- *"The trick is..."* as a shortcut to the key insight.
- Footnote a tangent rather than parenthetical-ize it.
- The *if-then* construction: *"If you want X, you have to do Y."*

**Lexical tells.** *I think, and yet, the trick is, the thing about, if you, people who.*

## Tim Urban (Wait But Why)

**Signature compositional move.** A parenthetical aside that contains the funniest or most insightful line in the paragraph. The aside is doing the work the prose itself is too polite to do.

**Opening sentence pattern.** Conversational. A confession, an embarrassing admission, or a direct address. *"OK so here's the weird thing..."* / *"I have a confession to make."* Often starts mid-thought, as if the reader walked in on him.

**Diction.** Colloquial. *Kind of, totally, this whole thing, the weird thing is.* Reaches for everyday metaphors before technical ones. Will say *the brain* not *the cerebral cortex*. Range goes high too — will use the technical term when needed, then immediately translate.

**Sentence shapes.** The widest variation in the canon. One-word fragments. Then a sentence that picks up seven commas and arrives at an unexpected place. Then ALL CAPS. The unevenness IS the music.

**Punctuation as voice.** Parentheses are load-bearing — often the longest construction in a paragraph is parenthetical. Em-dashes for emphasis. ALL CAPS sparingly, for mock alarm. Italics liberal. Footnotes that are themselves jokes (and that often contain footnotes within them).

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The parenthetical that is funnier than the main sentence.
- Sentence fragments for emphasis. Used carefully. Not too often.
- ALL CAPS as a controlled-volume knob.
- The extended metaphor as load-bearing structure — pick one and ride it for the whole piece.

**Lexical tells.** *OK so, the thing is, so basically, I'm telling you, kind of, the weird part is.*

## Morgan Housel

**Signature compositional move.** The single-sentence paragraph. Period. Then white space. Then another single-sentence paragraph. The whitespace IS the rhetoric.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A compressed historical anecdote, one or two sentences. Often a specific person doing something unexpected. *Never* a definition, never a statistic, never a question.

**Diction.** Plain. Numbers when they matter, words when they don't. Avoids financial jargon even when writing about finance. *Investor* not *capital allocator*.

**Sentence shapes.** Short. Often *very* short. Long sentences are rare and stand out when they appear. The whole piece is built from declarative bricks of roughly equal weight.

**Punctuation as voice.** Periods. Periods. Periods. Avoids em-dashes almost entirely. Avoids semicolons. The piece is built from short declaratives with white space between them.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The paradox construction: *"X, but Y."* / *"X, yet Y."*
- The one-sentence paragraph as the unit of thought.
- Numbers anchored in compression: *"A 100-hour project finishes. A 10,000-hour project never starts."*
- Historical anchor: open with a person doing a specific thing.

**Lexical tells.** *Few things are, the trick is, it's easy to, the funny thing is.*

## Ben Thompson (Stratechery)

**Signature compositional move.** A dense compound sentence introducing a named strategic frame, followed by deployment of that frame to a specific case. The named frame (*aggregation theory, distribution, multi-sided markets*) is the load-bearing word.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A news peg or recent corporate event, named specifically. Dense from sentence one. Assumes the reader is up to speed.

**Diction.** Business-specific. Reuses named strategic frames he's built up over years. Will write *the aggregator's incentive structure* rather than describe what the aggregator wants.

**Sentence shapes.** Medium-to-long. Compound sentences with multiple clauses. Consistent high density throughout — there's little air, but the prose doesn't quite suffocate because the analysis is moving.

**Punctuation as voice.** Heavy use of colons to introduce a frame or list. Em-dashes for parenthetical analytical asides. Block quotes from earnings calls, then dissected line by line.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The named frame as repeated load-bearing term.
- *"The question is..."* as a pivot to the analytical core.
- *"To put it another way..."* to restate the same observation from a different angle.
- The "...which is why..." conclusion construction.

**Lexical tells.** *The question is, what is striking is, to put it another way, the implications are clear.*

## David Brooks

**Signature compositional move.** A specific person in a specific place, observed, then a sentence beginning *"I think we are living through..."* that pivots from scene to cultural pattern.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A scene with a person — name, age, location, profession. *"I had coffee last month with a thirty-two-year-old engineer in Austin..."* The person is specific enough to be checkable.

**Diction.** Educated but accessible. Reaches for moral/character vocabulary without irony: *dignity, humility, longing, shame, vocation, calling, character.* This is unusual in modern columnists and is itself a fingerprint — most peers avoid these words for fear of seeming preachy.

**Sentence shapes.** Medium-leaning. A punchy short sentence will break up a paragraph of medium ones for rhythm.

**Punctuation as voice.** Standard newspaper. Nothing flashy. The prose's authority comes from its observed specifics, not its punctuation.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- *"I've come to believe..."* — the slow-arrived-at conclusion, signaling humility.
- Capitalized cultural-type names (*"The Resume Self," "The Eulogy Self"*).
- Reference to a specific real conversation as evidence.
- The settled, almost gentle, final sentence — the piece doesn't end with a kicker, it *lands*.

**Lexical tells.** *I've come to believe, I think we are living through, people who.*

## Ezra Klein

**Signature compositional move.** A long passage steelmanning the opposing view (often with a substantive quote from someone making it), followed by *"What I keep coming back to is..."* as a dialectical pivot.

**Opening sentence pattern.** Names the conventional wisdom or the position he'll argue against. Often quotes someone making the case — and quotes them respectfully.

**Diction.** Policy-wonk precise. Defines technical terms in passing. Quotes experts heavily.

**Sentence shapes.** Medium. Precise. Few rhetorical flourishes. The prose serves the argument; the argument is the show.

**Punctuation as voice.** Standard. Heavy on quotation marks (because heavy on quotes from experts). Em-dashes for clarification.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The steelman *before* the disagreement (and meaning it).
- *"I find this convincing — up to a point."* The point-where-I-stop-agreeing pivot.
- *"What I keep coming back to..."* as the dialectical kicker.
- Ending uncertain rather than faking certainty.

**Lexical tells.** *Here's the thing, what I keep coming back to, I want to take that seriously, and so, what I mean is.*

## Christopher Hitchens

**Signature compositional move.** A long Latinate sentence with multiple semicolon-linked clauses, the final clause landing a deflationary irony that undercuts the elevated diction it just used.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A literary or historical allusion deployed casually, as if everyone reading is on the same page. Auden, Orwell, Lenin, Connolly. The allusion does real argumentative work — it's not decoration.

**Diction.** Classical, literary. Latinate when it adds precision (which is often). Will use a 12-letter word when it's exactly the right word — and the rightness justifies it.

**Sentence shapes.** Long. Multi-clause. Semicolons connecting independent thoughts that build on each other. Never lost — the syntax is controlled even at length.

**Punctuation as voice.** Semicolons (the strongest Hitchens signature). Colons. Parenthetical asides done with comma-pairs, elegantly. Em-dashes for occasional conversational pivots.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The semicolon chain — multiple independent clauses in a single sentence, each building.
- *"One might note..."* / *"It is fashionable to suppose..."* as ironically diplomatic openers to brutal disagreements.
- The final *mot juste* — saving the most devastating word for the period position.
- A dry parenthetical that contains a sneer.

**Lexical tells.** *One might note, it is fashionable to suppose, the late, no doubt, in fairness.*

## Matt Levine

**Signature compositional move.** A parenthetical aside, often very long, that contains the actual joke or the actual point — while the main clause performs deadpan reportage. The parentheticals do the work.

**Opening sentence pattern.** An absurdity, an *imagine if* frame, or a deadpan summary of a corporate filing that the reader slowly realizes is insane. The setup poses as straight reportage.

**Diction.** Finance-specific but always explained. Dry irony throughout. Will use plain English to describe something Wall Street wraps in jargon (*"they took the money"*).

**Sentence shapes.** Medium-length core sentences with frequent (often very long) parenthetical asides that contain the funniest material in the piece.

**Punctuation as voice.** Parentheticals dominate (and this is the actual core of his voice). Em-dashes for surprise reveals. Footnotes that are themselves jokes — sometimes the joke takes three nested footnotes to land.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The parenthetical that is the actual argument while the main clause performs straight reportage.
- *"But anyway..."* as a self-aware transition from a digression.
- *"(I'm not making this up)"* parenthetical as the joke landing.
- A short flat sentence after a long absurd one, restating the absurdity with no comment.

**Lexical tells.** *But anyway, (I'm not making this up), the basic problem is, you know what, technically, look.*

## Naval Ravikant

**Signature compositional move.** A one-line claim. Period. Line break. Another one-line claim. Each sentence stands alone; the line breaks are doing the work paragraphs would otherwise do.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A one-line claim. Often paradoxical or counterintuitive. The first sentence IS the thesis.

**Diction.** Philosophical, terse. Abstractions made concrete by careful word choice. Avoids slang. Avoids most punctuation marks.

**Sentence shapes.** Short. Each sentence stands alone, almost as a tweet. Line breaks function as paragraphs.

**Punctuation as voice.** Periods, almost exclusively. Occasional colons. No em-dashes, no parentheticals, no semicolons. The compositional minimalism is the voice.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- The standalone aphorism as paragraph.
- The paradox structure as a one-liner: *"The bet is small. The asymmetry is large."*
- The *"X is Y"* declarative compression: *"A side project is a bet on yourself."*
- Closing with another aphorism rather than a summary — the piece runs out of necessary lines rather than concluding.

**Lexical tells.** *Specific knowledge, leverage, compounding, asymmetry, X is Y.*

## Anne Helen Petersen (cultural-essay mode)

**Signature compositional move.** *"I keep thinking about [specific recent cultural artifact]"* as an opener, pivoted by *"It's not just X — it's Y"* somewhere in the body.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A personal vignette or specific cultural artifact — a TikTok, an ad, an overheard exchange, an awkward text. Specific, recent, observed.

**Diction.** Academic-adjacent but readable. Names a cultural theorist or sociologist casually, as if everyone reading is on the same page. *Bourdieu, Hochschild, Ehrenreich.*

**Sentence shapes.** Medium to long. Leans literary. Will use a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis but doesn't lean on the move.

**Punctuation as voice.** Em-dashes liberal. Parentheticals for self-deprecation. Quotation marks around the genre's own terms (*"the grind," "the side hustle," "self-care"*) — the scare-quote is itself a position.

**Specific compositional moves to steal:**
- *"I keep thinking about..."* as a permission slip for a personal observation.
- *"It's not just X — it's Y"* as the analytical pivot.
- Naming a cultural theorist casually mid-paragraph, without explanation.
- Scare quotes around genre terms to signal critique without arguing for it.
- The closer that returns to the opening vignette with new meaning.

**Lexical tells.** *I keep thinking about, what we talk about when we talk about, it's not just X — it's Y.*

## Blending voices within the opinion-essay tradition

The strongest pieces are usually blends *within a register*. Good blends:
- **Housel paragraph rhythm + Graham first-principles reasoning** — short paragraphs that each prove a logical step. Great for *how-to-think* essays.
- **Thompson backbone + Levine asides** — strategic analysis with dry irony in the parentheticals. Great for tech business commentary.
- **Brooks scene-setting lede + Klein dialectical body** — start with a person, end with a balanced argument. Great for cultural-political pieces.
- **Hitchens stance + Levine humor** — combative position softened by self-aware wit. Great for takedowns that don't read as bitter.
- **Petersen opening + Naval kicker** — cultural vignette landing on an aphorism. Great for short newsletter pieces.

Bad blends (these fight at the line level):
- **Hitchens diction + Tim Urban parentheticals** — the literary register and the conversational register will produce something that reads like a person having a stroke.
- **Naval compression + Brooks moral framing** — Naval needs white space; Brooks needs scenes to develop. They cancel each other.
- **Housel period-only minimalism + Levine parenthetical maximalism** — opposite punctuation philosophies.

When picking a blend, name both writers and what each contributes compositionally — not just vibes.

# Part II — Other registers

These registers are *not* the opinion-essay tradition above. Compositionally they obey different rules. Don't blend across the line between Part I and Part II without good reason — you'll produce hybrid prose that doesn't belong to any genre.

## Narrative journalism / feature longform

Informed by Hannah Dreier, Gene Weingarten, and Lane DeGregory in `user-canon/`. Use when the piece is **reported**, not opined.

**Signature compositional move.** Open with a scene so specific it places the reader in a room before the piece's argument is named. The thesis arrives mid-piece, after the reader is invested in one person.

**Opening sentence pattern.** An existential opener (*"It was almost midnight in..."*), a delayed-reveal sentence (key noun saved for the period position), or an anti-glamorous physical detail. All three withhold the central fact until late in the sentence or piece.

**Diction.** Plain. Reportorial. Anglo-Saxon-leaning. Avoids evaluative adjectives — when something is horrible, the writer shows the specific horror (cockroaches in the freezer; a woman's scalp ripped open) and lets the reader feel it. Names, brands, places, and titles all specific and real.

**Sentence shapes.** Subject-verb-object dominant. Few embedded clauses. The piece moves fast at the sentence level. Short sentences land physical specifics; longer sentences carry scope.

**Punctuation as voice.** Quiet. Standard newspaper. Em-dashes rare and pointed. Periods do the work.

**Specific compositional moves:**
- The agent-of-the-verb-is-the-system construction: *"A conveyor belt carried bags of Cheerios past a cluster of young workers."* The belt acts; the workers are acted upon. Grammar as editorial choice.
- Compressed biography in a single trailing clause, with the heaviest fact saved for the period position.
- Specific brand names as accountability nouns.
- Quotes that characterize the speaker rather than informing the reader.

**Don't blend with:** opinion-essay registers (the reportorial flatness will collide with the opinion writer's commitment), academic register (would feel cold).

## Personal-memoir essay (literary-magazine register)

Informed by Bryan Burrough's "Vanity Fair's Heyday" in `user-canon/`. Use when the piece is fundamentally *I was there, and here's what it was like.*

**Signature compositional move.** Two registers in alternation within a single sentence — literary phrase next to colloquial aside next to specific name. The texture is the voice.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A self-aware confession or threat to the reader. *"If I share my part of its story accurately, you will probably hate me."*

**Diction — central move.** Literary register *and* colloquial register, switched mid-sentence:
- Literary: *halcyon era, winged impresario hair, Rosetta stone for literary archeologists, pickled British iconoclast.*
- Colloquial: *cards on the table, What can I say?, ring your bell, scuttle away.*

The alternation flatters both. The literary signals authority; the colloquial signals intimacy. Reader feels simultaneously talked-up-to and talked-with.

**Sentence shapes.** Long, multi-clause, em-dash-rich sentences interspersed with short declarative punches as one-line paragraphs. *"Also, dead bodies. Lots of bodies."* The contrast is the music.

**Punctuation as voice.** Em-dashes everywhere (the central signature). Parentheticals for intimacy (*"(I am grateful that none of this appears in the book.)"*). Colons sparingly. Italics for titles.

**Specific compositional moves:**
- The absurd-specific number: *"sold about seventeen copies"* (not "very few").
- Named-specifics-as-texture (Leibovitz, Princess Margaret, Jimmy Choos), not as reference.
- Self-deprecating clauses adjacent to achievements.
- Anaphoric closer: *"I never... I never... Not once."*
- Parentheticals for intimate asides.

**Lexical tells.** *Halcyon, What can I say?, Cards on the table, If you knew you knew, Yes I checked the index first, boldface names.*

**Blends within Part II:**
- *Personal-memoir + narrative-journalism (light touch)* — works when the writer is briefly part of the story. Burrough does this.
- *Personal-memoir + Brooks moral framing* — works because both are reflective. Don't push it; Burrough is wittier than Brooks.

## Academic / management thought-leadership

Informed by the (untitled) AI/organizational-intelligence article in `user-canon/`. **Use rarely.** Most columnist work should explicitly avoid this register. Listed here so the skill can recognize it, deploy it when genuinely warranted (white papers, academic journals, MBA application essays, some thought-leadership for HBR/McKinsey audiences), and diagnose when a draft is unintentionally drifting into it.

**Signature compositional move.** A defined-term loop — the writer introduces a hyphenated technical phrase (*intelligence-in-use*, *actor composition*) and then references it as an established entity for the rest of the piece. The vocabulary is the credential.

**Opening sentence pattern.** A meta-statement (*"This article presents..."*) under an "Abstract" heading. The piece announces what it will do rather than doing it.

**Diction.** Latinate. *Implementation, augmentation, leveraging, complementary, organizational.* When an Anglo-Saxon option exists, the academic register chooses the Latinate one because it signals seriousness. Heavy nominalization — verbs turned into nouns.

**Sentence shapes.** Long. Heavily nominalized. Subject-verb distance large. Multiple subordinate clauses. Cross-references to numbered items (*"as specified in equation 1"*).

**Punctuation as voice.** Numbered citations¹ embedded² in³ the prose⁴ (the dominant signature). Em-dashes for lists. Parentheses for definitions and citations only, never for asides. Bullet lists for formal enumerations.

**Specific compositional moves *that can transfer* (used sparingly in columnist work):**
- The *"I define X as Y"* move when introducing a specific concept.
- Citation density to build authority (one or two named experts per column, not eight per paragraph).
- The hypothetical illustration when a real example isn't available.

**Specific moves to *never* import into columnist work:**
- The meta-statement opening.
- Nominalization stacks.
- Inline numbered citations.
- The *actor* abstraction for any agent.
- Equations or formal models in body prose.
- Defined-term loops.
- Bullet lists in body prose.

**Diagnostic.** If a draft contains any of: a sentence beginning *"This piece presents..."*, the word *leveraging* as a verb, more than one citation per paragraph, or a defined technical term that the reader has to remember the meaning of — the writer has drifted into the academic register and Phase 7 (Tighten) should pull it back.

**Lexical tells.** *Comprehensive, constituting, implementation, augmentation, intelligence-in-use, actor, leveraging, complementary, paradigm, framework.*

**Blends:** Almost none. This register is genre-locked. The closest blend that works is *light academic flavor inside a Ben Thompson voice* — Thompson borrows the *named-frame* move from this register but writes the rest in columnist prose. That's the only safe import.

# Reading these in sequence

When picking a voice in Phase 3:

1. **First identify the register.** Is the piece opined or reported? Personal-memoir? Academic? The register decision is bigger than the voice decision; voices blend within registers but rarely across them.
2. **Then pick a primary voice within the register.** Or a blend of two voices known to compose well together (see the blend lists above).
3. **Name the voice (or blend) in the trace, and name *what each contributes compositionally* — not just vibes.** "Housel rhythm + Graham first-principles" is a real diagnosis; "thoughtful and clear" is not.
4. **Scan `exemplar-pastiches.md` to absorb the cadence** before drafting, especially when blending voices that you don't write in often.
5. **Scan the relevant `user-canon/` entries** — the composition notes there are deeper than the fingerprints above. The fingerprints are the index; the canon notes are the chapter.
