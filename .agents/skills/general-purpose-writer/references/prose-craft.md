# Prose Craft — the sentence-level toolkit

Depth reference for `[[general-purpose-writer]]`. Read when you're shaping prose at the line level or diagnosing why a passage feels flat.

## Rhythm and the music of sentences
- **Vary length on purpose.** A run of same-length sentences flattens into monotone. Build long sentences that accumulate clauses and breath and forward pressure — then cut to something short. The short one hits hardest after a long one. (This is the oldest move in English prose and it still works.)
- **End on the strong word.** The last word of a sentence carries the most weight; the first carries the second-most. Bury the weak words in the middle. "He died, alone and afraid, in a borrowed room" — feel where the stress falls versus "In a borrowed room he died alone and afraid."
- **Use the period.** Fragments, used sparingly and deliberately, punch. So do short declaratives after complexity.
- **Mind the syllables and stresses.** Prose has meter even when it isn't verse. Trochaic, iambic runs; a cluster of monosyllables slows the reader down ("and the slow, cold, gray light"); polysyllables speed and smooth. Read aloud; let your ear, not a rule, decide.
- **Punctuation is tempo.** The comma is a short breath; the semicolon yokes two equals; the colon promises and delivers; the dash interrupts and dramatizes; the period is a full stop and a small silence. Choose for rhythm, not just grammar.

## Diction — choosing the word
- **Concrete and specific beats abstract and general.** Not "vegetation" but "nettles." Not "she was emotional" but "she laughed at the funeral." Specificity is credibility; it convinces the reader you were there.
- **Anglo-Saxon over Latinate for force; Latinate for abstraction and ceremony.** "Help" lands harder than "facilitate"; "begin" beats "commence." Reach for the Latinate register only when the subject or tone genuinely calls for it.
- **Strong verbs carry sentences.** Spend your effort here. Replace "make better" with "sharpen," "is a reflection of" with "mirrors." Most adverbs are a confession that the verb is weak.
- **Avoid the fashionable filler:** utilize, leverage, delve, robust, seamless, tapestry, testament, realm, landscape, journey (metaphorical), navigate (metaphorical). They signal prose written on autopilot.
- **One precise word beats two approximate ones.** Don't hedge a noun with an adjective that apologizes for it.

## Syntax — sentence architecture
- **Default to actor–action–object.** Who did what to whom. The passive voice hides the actor; use it only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately suppressed.
- **Subject and verb close together.** The longer the gap, the more the reader has to hold in memory. Keep the spine tight; hang the detail off the ends.
- **Periodic vs. loose.** A *periodic* sentence withholds its main clause to the end, building suspense ("Through the rain, past the shuttered shops, not once looking back, she walked"). A *loose* sentence states its point and trails detail. Periodic for drama and emphasis; loose for ease and speed. Alternate.
- **Parallelism for power, broken parallelism for surprise.** Matched structures ("I came, I saw, I conquered") create rhythm and authority. Then break the pattern when you want the reader to jolt.

## Imagery and the senses
- **Write to the five senses.** Most flat prose is purely visual or purely abstract. Smell and sound and touch are underused and disproportionately vivid.
- **The telling detail.** One exact, slightly unexpected particular does more than a paragraph of description. The character isn't "nervous" — he keeps re-aligning the stapler on his desk.
- **Show, then trust.** Render the scene and let the reader conclude. Don't show *and* tell; the explanation kills what the image earned.

## Metaphor and figurative language
- **Fresh or none.** A live metaphor reveals; a dead one ("the wheels of progress") is wallpaper. If the comparison is one you've read before, cut it or remake it.
- **Keep it coherent.** Don't mix metaphors ("a virgin field pregnant with possibilities"). Once you choose an image, stay inside its world for the length of the figure.
- **Don't over-figure.** A single strong metaphor per passage outshines three competing ones. Restraint is taste.

## Point of view and distance
- **Fix the POV and hold it.** First person (intimate, fallible), close third (inside one head), distant third (the camera pulls back), second person (implicating, risky). Drifting between them disorients the reader.
- **Control psychic distance.** You can stand far ("It was 1924 and the town was small") or press close ("God, the boy was tired"). Move in and out deliberately within a scene; don't lurch.

## Tone and register
- Tone is the writer's attitude to the subject and reader: warm, ironic, elegiac, brisk, reverent, deadpan. Decide it before you draft and keep it consistent — a single sentence in the wrong register breaks the spell.
- Register is the formality level — from gutter to pulpit. Match it to form and audience, and shift it only for deliberate effect (a sudden plain word in ornate prose, or vice versa, can be a thunderclap).

## A few load-bearing maxims
- "Omit needless words." (Strunk) — but not all words; rhythm sometimes needs the extra beat.
- "Find the action and put it in the verb." (Williams)
- "Write drunk, edit sober" is a lie about drafting and a truth about distance: draft loose, revise cold.
- "No tears in the writer, no tears in the reader." (Frost) — if you feel nothing writing it, the reader will feel nothing reading it.
