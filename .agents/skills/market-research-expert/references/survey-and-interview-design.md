# Survey & Interview Design (Primary Research)

Primary research resolves the unknowns secondary data can't. The whole value is in **unbiased instruments**: a leading question manufactures the answer you wanted and teaches you nothing. Decide first what decision the data will inform and what result would change it (→ SKILL.md step 1).

## Choose the instrument
- **Interviews (qualitative)** — *why* and *how*; discover unknown unknowns, the job, the story. 5–8 per segment usually surfaces the dominant patterns (diminishing returns after ~the point you can predict answers).
- **Surveys (quantitative)** — *how many* / *how much*; size and rank what you already roughly understand. Need adequate, representative n.
- **Sequence:** interviews first to learn the language and hypotheses → survey to quantify at scale. Surveying before you understand the space produces precise answers to the wrong questions.

## Customer interviews

### Golden rules
- **Ask about the past, not the future.** "Tell me about the last time you [did the job]" → real behavior. "Would you use…?" → polite fiction.
- **Talk about their life and problem, not your idea** (The Mom Test). Don't pitch; if they know you're selling, they'll be nice and useless.
- **Dig for specifics:** when did it last happen, what did you do, what did it cost you (time/money/frustration), what did you try, why didn't that work, what happened next.
- **Listen for commitment & currency** — have they spent time/money/reputation trying to solve it? Talk is cheap; effort is signal.
- **Shut up.** Ask open question, then silence. Aim for them talking ~80% of the time. Follow "why?" 3–5 levels.

### Interview guide skeleton
```
1. Warm-up / context: their role, their world (2 min)
2. The job & last occurrence: "Walk me through the last time you [job]."
3. Current solution & workarounds: "What do you use today? What's annoying about it?"
4. Pain & cost: "What's the hardest part? What does that cost you (time/money)?"
5. Triggers & alternatives: "What made you look for a fix? What else did you try? Why'd you stop?"
6. (Only at the end, if at all) reaction to concept — and treat verbal enthusiasm skeptically
7. Wrap: "What didn't I ask that I should have? Who else should I talk to?"
```
Record (with consent) or take verbatim notes; quote exact phrasing — it becomes JTBD + positioning language. Log each interview as a source (date, segment).

### Interview anti-patterns
- Pitching instead of listening; seeking compliments.
- Hypotheticals and leading prompts ("Wouldn't it be great if…?").
- Accepting "yes I'd buy it" without a commitment ask.
- Talking to anyone reachable instead of the actual target segment.
- Confirmation bias — only recording what supports the idea.

## Surveys

### Before writing a single question
- Define the **decision** and the **analysis plan**: write the chart/number each question will produce. If a question doesn't feed a decision, cut it.
- Define **population & sampling**: who qualifies, how you'll reach them, target n. Note the sampling bias (e.g., "recruited from our newsletter → skews existing fans").
- Keep it short (≤5 min). Length kills completion and quality.

### Question-writing rules (avoid bias)
- **One idea per question.** Split double-barreled questions ("Is it fast and reliable?").
- **Neutral wording.** No loaded adjectives, no presupposition. ❌ "How much do you love feature X?" ✅ "How satisfied or dissatisfied are you with X?" (balanced scale).
- **Balanced scales.** Equal positive/negative options, labeled, with a neutral midpoint and an "N/A / don't know." Avoid forcing a stance.
- **No leading or anchoring.** Don't state a number/opinion before asking for theirs (esp. pricing — never name a price first).
- **Avoid acquiescence bias** — people agree with statements; prefer specific behavioral questions over "Do you agree that…?"
- **Closed for measuring, open for discovering.** A few open boxes capture language; keep them optional.
- **Match recall to reality.** Ask about recent, concrete behavior ("in the last 30 days"), not lifetime averages.
- **Randomize** answer-option order where order effects matter; pilot for **social-desirability bias** (sensitive topics get over-reported "good" behavior).

### Pricing questions in a survey
- **Van Westendorp PSM** — ask all four, in order: at what price is it *so cheap you'd doubt quality* / *a bargain* / *getting expensive* / *too expensive to consider*? → acceptable price band.
- **Gabor-Granger** — present discrete price points, measure purchase likelihood at each → demand curve & revenue-max point.
- Never lead with a single price ("Would you pay $20?") — it anchors and inflates.

### Bias checklist (run before sending)
- [ ] Every question maps to a decision / planned chart.
- [ ] No double-barreled questions.
- [ ] No leading/loaded wording; scales balanced and labeled.
- [ ] No anchoring (esp. price) before the respondent answers.
- [ ] Screening questions ensure respondents are the target population.
- [ ] Sampling bias acknowledged and noted in the writeup.
- [ ] Survey piloted with 3–5 people; confusing items fixed.
- [ ] Length ≤ ~5 min.

### Reporting survey results honestly
State **n**, the sampling method, the **margin of error / "directional only"** caveat for small/biased samples, and the dates fielded. Don't over-read a non-representative convenience sample — present it as a qualitative signal, not a population statistic. Tag inferences vs measured results.

## Anti-patterns (both methods)
- Leading questions that manufacture the desired answer.
- Asking about the imagined future instead of observed past.
- Treating a tiny, self-selected sample as statistically representative.
- Naming a price first and calling the echo "validated WTP."
- Running a survey before interviews have taught you what to ask.
- Reporting percentages without n, method, and date.
