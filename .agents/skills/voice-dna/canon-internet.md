# Canon: Internet, Aphoristic & Most-Shared

The voice models for writing that lives and spreads online: the clarity of the great internet essayists, the compression of the aphorists, and the pieces ordinary humans have loved enough to forward to everyone they know. Different register from the literary canon, faster, more direct, more useful, but the same core values: specificity, rhythm, zero throat-clearing.

**A note on balance:** this set leans libertarian and market-friendly by nature, the great internet essayists (Graham, Naval, Housel, Kelly, Sivers) mostly write from a center-right, free-market sensibility, which broadens the skill's range past the literary canon's progressive tilt. Andrew Sullivan adds an explicitly heterodox-conservative voice. As always, study the craft, not the politics; and for writing that reaches across the divide on purpose, see register K in the longform canon.

**How to use this file:** Match your intent in the map, jump to the section, steal the move. These voices are especially right for newsletters, posts, threads, founder essays, and anything meant to be read on a phone and shared.

## H. The Internet Essay / Clarity & System-Thinking

The most-read nonfiction of the web. Take one hard idea and make it feel obvious. Conversational but not sloppy. Short paragraphs. Concrete examples over theory. The reader should finish feeling smarter, not lectured.

### Paul Graham — the essays
- **Where:** paulgraham.com. Start with "How to Do Great Work" (2023, https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html), "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" (2009, https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html), "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html).
- **Voice DNA:** Plain words, short sentences, one idea per paragraph. He thinks *on the page*, you watch the reasoning happen instead of getting a conclusion handed to you. No jargon, no hedging, no decoration. The authority comes from clarity, not vocabulary.
- **Move to steal:** Strip every sentence to its load-bearing words. If a paragraph holds two ideas, split it. Make the reader feel the click of understanding, don't describe the click.

### Tim Urban (Wait But Why) — "The Tail End"
- **Where:** waitbutwhy.com, December 2015 (https://waitbutwhy.com/2015/12/the-tail-end.html). See also "Why Procrastinators Procrastinate" (2013).
- **Voice DNA:** Takes an abstract dread (mortality, time with aging parents) and renders it as literal counted boxes, you *see* the weeks you have left. Stick-figure logic: break the huge thing into a number you can hold. Warm, funny, then quietly devastating.
- **Move to steal:** Convert a vague feeling into a hard count. "90% of the time you'll ever spend with your parents is already gone" beats any adjective about time being precious.

### Paul Ford — "What Is Code?"
- **Where:** Bloomberg Businessweek, June 2015 (a 38,000-word cover story, https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2015-paul-ford-what-is-code/).
- **Voice DNA:** Explains all of software to a non-technical executive with patience, humor, and second-person intimacy ("you"). Makes an enormous, dry subject feel like a guided tour with a witty friend. Never condescends, never oversimplifies.
- **Move to steal:** Address one specific imagined reader as "you" and guide them by the hand. Intimacy scales; a lecture to "people" reaches no one.

### Patrick McKenzie (patio11) — "Salary Negotiation"
- **Where:** kalzumeus.com, 2012 (https://www.kalzumeus.com/2012/01/23/salary-negotiation/).
- **Voice DNA:** Dense, generous, almost aggressively useful, every paragraph pays rent in actionable advice. Reframes a scary topic ("negotiation is adversarial") into a calm, learnable game. Authority through sheer specificity of the tactics.
- **Move to steal:** Make every paragraph give the reader something they can *do*. Usefulness is the most shareable quality on the internet.

### Andrew Sullivan — "The Daily Dish" / the column
- **Where:** pioneered political blogging at The Daily Dish (2000s); now "The Weekly Dish" on Substack. A gay, Catholic, Oakeshott-quoting conservative who broke with the right as often as the left.
- **Voice DNA:** Argues in real time and in public, thinks out loud, changes his mind on the page, and tells you when he was wrong. Personal conviction plus intellectual honesty. The heterodoxy is the appeal: readers trust someone who criticizes his own side.
- **Move to steal:** Show your reasoning live, including the doubts and the reversals. A writer who visibly updates earns more trust than one who only ever sounds certain.

## I. Aphoristic / Sticky / Compression

Built to be remembered and repeated. The unit is the line, not the paragraph. Maximum meaning, minimum words. The Twitter-era art of the screenshot. Hard to do well, easy to do badly (see: every fortune-cookie LinkedIn post).

### Naval Ravikant — "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)"
- **Where:** Tweetstorm, 2018 (collected in *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*).
- **Voice DNA:** Each line is a self-contained, tweet-sized axiom that survives on its own. Compression to the point of koan. "Play long-term games with long-term people." No connective tissue, the gaps are load-bearing, the reader fills them.
- **Move to steal:** Write the idea as a standalone line that needs no sentence before or after it. If it only works in context, it isn't sticky yet. Cut until it stands alone.

### Kevin Kelly — "1,000 True Fans" / "68 Bits of Unsolicited Advice"
- **Where:** kk.org / The Technium, 2008 (https://kk.org/thetechnium/1000-true-fans/) and 2020 (the "68 Bits" birthday post).
- **Voice DNA:** Counterintuitive idea stated plainly, then made undeniable with simple arithmetic (1,000 fans × $100 = a living). The advice posts are pure compression, one clean sentence per line, no padding. Warmth without sentimentality.
- **Move to steal:** Back a counterintuitive claim with one line of arithmetic the reader can't argue with. Math is the most persuasive form of brevity.

### Morgan Housel — "The Psychology of Money"
- **Where:** Collaborative Fund blog, 2018 (the post that became the book).
- **Voice DNA:** Short, story-driven sections, each a single behavioral truth illustrated by one vivid anecdote. Plainspoken, never preachy. He trusts a small story to carry a big idea, then gets out of the way.
- **Move to steal:** One idea, one story, then stop. Resist the urge to explain the moral, the story already did it. The restraint is the craft.

### Derek Sivers — "Hell Yeah or No"
- **Where:** sive.rs (https://sive.rs/hyon).
- **Voice DNA:** Radical brevity, posts are sometimes three sentences. Every word fights for its life. The whitespace is part of the message. Proof that "short" and "deep" aren't opposites.
- **Move to steal:** Try writing it in a third of the length. The discipline of extreme cutting almost always sharpens the idea, not just the prose.

## J. Most-Shared by Humans / The Viral Heart

The pieces real people forwarded to their families, taped to the fridge, read at weddings and funerals. Not the most awarded, the most *loved*. They work because they're emotionally true and effortless to pass along. Study what makes a stranger think of someone they love and hit "send."

### Mary Schmich — "Wear Sunscreen"
- **Where:** Chicago Tribune, June 1, 1997, real title "Advice, like youth, probably just wasted on the young." One of the internet's first viral texts; spread by email, misattributed to Kurt Vonnegut as an MIT speech, turned into a hit song. Schmich won the 2012 Pulitzer for Commentary.
- **Voice DNA:** A mock commencement speech: tiny concrete advice ("wear sunscreen") braided with the big stuff ("don't worry about the future"). The structure, a list of small specific dos and don'ts, makes wisdom feel light and quotable instead of heavy. Each line is independently forwardable.
- **Move to steal:** Anchor big life-wisdom to one absurdly small, concrete piece of advice. The specific tiny thing is the hook the whole message hangs on, and the reason people remember it.

### Tim Kreider — "The Busy Trap"
- **Where:** The New York Times, June 30, 2012, one of the paper's most-shared opinion pieces. Link: https://opinionator.blogs.nytimes.com/2012/06/30/the-busy-trap/
- **Voice DNA:** Names a feeling everyone has but hadn't articulated, "busyness as a hedge against emptiness", and gives it a sharp phrase ("a boast disguised as a complaint"). Personal, a little contrarian, lands a diagnosis the reader instantly recognizes about themselves.
- **Move to steal:** Find the thing everyone feels but no one has named, then name it in one crisp phrase. People share the words that finally describe their own life.

### William Deresiewicz — "Solitude and Leadership"
- **Where:** The American Scholar, 2010 (adapted from a lecture to West Point cadets, https://theamericanscholar.org/solitude-and-leadership/). See also "The Disadvantages of an Elite Education" (2008), one of the most-read essays the magazine ever published.
- **Voice DNA:** Speaks directly to a specific audience (cadets) about one counterintuitive claim, that real leadership requires solitude and the courage to think alone. Conversational lecture rhythm, builds the argument like he's talking *to you*. Earnest without being soft.
- **Move to steal:** Pick one counterintuitive thesis and argue it to one specific audience, out loud, in the second person. The directness is what makes it travel beyond the room.

### Bronnie Ware — "Top Five Regrets of the Dying"
- **Where:** Personal blog, 2009 (became the 2011 book; the original post was shared millions of times).
- **Voice DNA:** A palliative-care nurse simply lists what dying people told her they regretted. The power is total restraint, she reports, she doesn't editorialize, and the list does everything. Plain language, devastating content, no manipulation.
- **Move to steal:** When the material is already powerful, get out of its way. A clean list of true things hits harder than any amount of writerly framing. Don't add violins.

### Rebecca Solnit — "Men Explain Things to Me"
- **Where:** TomDispatch, 2008 (the essay that seeded the word "mansplaining," though Solnit didn't coin it). Collected 2014.
- **Voice DNA:** Opens with one perfect, funny, specific anecdote (a man condescendingly explaining her own book to her) and lets it bloom into a structural argument. The story is so precise and recognizable it became cultural shorthand.
- **Move to steal:** Lead with the one anecdote so specific and recognizable that readers think "that happened to me too." Universality is reached through the hyper-specific, never the general.
