# Canon: Longform & Literary

The voice models for serious nonfiction: profiles, features, criticism, polemic, lyric essay. Each entry analyzes the *technique* that makes the piece resonate, then hands you the one move worth stealing. These are reference snapshots, not reproductions, read the originals at the links to absorb the full voice.

**This canon spans the political spectrum on purpose.** Great prose is not owned by the left or the right, and a writer who only studies one side learns half the craft. You'll find progressive flagship essays (Coates, Morris, Baldwin) next to conservative and libertarian masters (Buckley, Scalia, Noonan, O'Rourke), plus a whole register on writing that persuades people who already disagree with you (register K). Study every voice for its *technique*, not its politics. Many of these writers resist easy labels anyway, Didion started on the right, Wolfe spent a career puncturing liberal pieties, Orwell is claimed by both camps.

**How to use this file:** Don't read it cover to cover. Find the register that matches your intent in the map below, jump to that section, and pull the move. One voice per draft, usually. Mixing two is how you get mush.

## A. Immersive Narrative / Scene-Driven Feature

The reader should forget they're reading. You vanish; the scene does the work. Show, never summarize. Detail is argument.

### Gay Talese — "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold"
- **Where:** Esquire, April 1966. Widely called the greatest magazine story ever written. Link: https://www.esquire.com/news-politics/a638/frank-sinatra-has-a-cold-gay-talese/
- **Voice DNA:** Talese never got an interview with Sinatra. So he wrote the orbit instead, the bodyguards, the hangers-on, the woman who carries his toupees. The absence becomes the portrait. Hyper-observed physical detail (the gray of a sleeve, who flinches when) carries every ounce of meaning.
- **Move to steal:** When you can't get the center, report the orbit. What surrounds a person tells you more than the person would.

### Eli Saslow — "Anger and heartbreak on Bus No. 15"
- **Where:** The Washington Post, June 6, 2022. Part of the body of work that won the 2023 Pulitzer for Feature Writing. Link: https://www.washingtonpost.com/nation/2022/06/06/bus-denver-pendemic-violence/
- **Voice DNA:** Opens on a character mid-action, a bus driver touching up makeup in the rearview mirror, and lets dialogue reveal the trauma so the narrator never has to announce it. Precise timestamps ("5:32 a.m.") do the work of a thousand adjectives.
- **Move to steal:** Open on a person already in motion, doing one small concrete thing. Trust the reader to feel the weight without being told it's heavy.

### Susan Orlean — "The American Man, at Age Ten"
- **Where:** Esquire, 1992 (collected in *The Bullfighter Checks Her Makeup*).
- **Voice DNA:** Orlean profiles an ordinary 10-year-old boy with the gravity usually reserved for presidents, and the mismatch is the magic. Curiosity without condescension. She takes her small subject completely seriously and the reader does too.
- **Move to steal:** Take a "small" subject dead seriously. The respect you grant it is what makes it large.

### Tom Junod — "The Falling Man"
- **Where:** Esquire, September 2003.
- **Voice DNA:** Builds an entire 9/11 meditation around the identity of one man in one photograph. Restraint under unbearable material, the prose stays still while the subject is in free fall. The discipline is the emotion.
- **Move to steal:** When the material is overwhelming, write quieter, not louder. Stillness on the page makes the reader's own reaction the loudest thing in the room.

## B. Explanatory / Make-the-Complex-Visceral

The reader has to *feel* the abstract thing in their body, not just understand it. Analogy is your scalpel. Pacing is your weapon.

### Kathryn Schulz — "The Really Big One"
- **Where:** The New Yorker, July 13/20, 2015. Won the 2016 Pulitzer for Feature Writing. Background interview: https://www.theopennotebook.com/2015/09/15/kathryn-schulz-the-really-big-one/
- **Voice DNA:** Turns Cascadia-fault geology into dread you can hold in your hands, literally: hold your palms down, middle fingertips touching, one hand is a tectonic plate. The science becomes a body. High-stakes pacing keeps the catastrophe always one sentence away.
- **Move to steal:** Translate an invisible system into something the reader can do with their own hands. Make them perform the metaphor.

### David Foster Wallace — "Roger Federer as Religious Experience"
- **Where:** The New York Times (Play magazine), August 20, 2006. Link: https://www.nytimes.com/2006/08/20/sports/playmagazine/20federer.html
- **Voice DNA:** Marries breathless, footnote-laden technical precision (the exact geometry of a passing shot) to genuine transcendence. The "Federer Moments" framing gives a shapeless feeling a name and a structure. Hyper-specificity *is* the reverence.
- **Move to steal:** Name the ineffable thing ("a Federer Moment"), then earn the name with absurdly precise mechanics. Awe + engineering.

## C. Cultural Criticism / Personal-Meets-Political

The "I" is a lens, not the subject. You move between low culture and high stakes, between a song and four hundred years. The personal reveal lands because you earned it.

### Wesley Morris — "The Song That Knows Our Rage"
- **Where:** The New York Times, June 2020. Part of the work that won the 2021 Pulitzer for Criticism. Interview: https://www.cjr.org/podcast/wesley-morris-floyd-labelle-racism.php
- **Voice DNA:** Starts doubled over a kitchen sink, wrecked by Patti LaBelle's 1985 cover of "If You Don't Know Me by Now," and pulls four hundred years of Black suffering out of one vocal performance. Alternates "playful" and "profound" sentence by sentence. The intimacy is the authority.
- **Move to steal:** Anchor a vast argument in one small, embarrassing, specific personal moment. The reader trusts the big claim because you showed them the small bruise.

### David Foster Wallace — "Consider the Lobster"
- **Where:** Gourmet, August 2004.
- **Voice DNA:** A food magazine sends him to a lobster festival; he files a treatise on whether it's okay to boil a creature alive. The structural joke, philosophy in the wrong venue, is the engine. Footnotes argue with the main text.
- **Move to steal:** Smuggle the serious question inside the trivial assignment. The contrast does half the persuasion for you.

### James Baldwin — "Notes of a Native Son"
- **Where:** Harper's, 1955 (title essay of the 1955 collection).
- **Voice DNA:** Braids his father's funeral, Harlem riots, and his own rage into a single sustained sentence-music that builds like a sermon. Long, rolling, cumulative clauses, then a short hammer-blow line. Moral seriousness without a shred of preachiness.
- **Move to steal:** Build long, then cut short. Three rolling sentences that accumulate, then one four-word line that lands the blow.

## D. Provocation / The Punchy Critic

Short declarative sentences. Stake a big claim and dare the reader to disagree. Unpretentious, fast, certain. The opposite of throat-clearing.

### Jerry Saltz — "Christie's Says This Painting Is by Leonardo. I Doubt It."
- **Where:** New York Magazine / Vulture, November 14, 2017 (the day before the Salvator Mundi auction). The painting sold for $450.3 million.
- **Voice DNA:** Punchy, unpretentious, provocative. Stakes a $100-million claim in one-clause sentences. "I have doubts. Big doubts." The everyman register makes the high-art verdict feel like common sense.
- **Move to steal:** Make the big call in the shortest possible sentences. Confidence reads as clarity; hedging reads as fear.

### Anthony Bourdain — "Don't Eat Before Reading This"
- **Where:** The New Yorker, April 1999 (the piece that became *Kitchen Confidential*).
- **Voice DNA:** Insider swagger plus genuine warning. He's letting you behind the kitchen door and the thrill is that he shouldn't be. Profane, specific, generous. Voice so strong it launched a second career.
- **Move to steal:** Write like you're telling a secret you're not supposed to tell. Insider specificity (don't order fish on Mondays) buys total credibility.

## E. Argument / Polemic / Moral Clarity

You're here to change a mind, not perform balance. Clarity is the ethic. Evidence is the muscle. The sentence that can't be misread is the goal. These voices deliberately span left and right, Orwell and Coates on one side, Buckley, Scalia, and Krauthammer on the other, because the craft of argument is identical whichever side you're arguing from.

### George Orwell — "Politics and the English Language"
- **Where:** Horizon, 1946.
- **Voice DNA:** Argues that vague writing enables bad politics, and proves it by being savagely clear. Concrete over abstract, short Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate fog, the active voice as a moral position. His six rules are still the spine of every style guide worth keeping.
- **Move to steal:** Never reach for a long word where a short one works, or the passive where the active will do. Foggy language hides weak thinking; clear language has nowhere to hide.

### Ta-Nehisi Coates — "The Case for Reparations"
- **Where:** The Atlantic, June 2014 (~16,000 words). Named NYU's "Top Work of Journalism of the Decade." Link: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2014/06/the-case-for-reparations/361631/
- **Voice DNA:** Opens with hammering anaphora, "Two hundred fifty years of slavery. Ninety years of Jim Crow.", stacking eras until the weight is undeniable. Then grounds the macro argument in one man, Clyde Ross, so the policy history has a face.
- **Move to steal:** Use anaphora (repeated sentence openings) to accumulate weight, then immediately anchor it in a single named human so the abstraction can't float away.

### Christopher Hitchens — "Topic of Cancer"
- **Where:** Vanity Fair, September 2010.
- **Voice DNA:** Writes about his own terminal esophageal cancer with zero self-pity and full wit, deploying his enemy, "the country called cancer", as a travel metaphor. Erudition worn lightly. The intelligence never tips into showing off because it's always in service of the point.
- **Move to steal:** Refuse the obvious emotion. On the heaviest subject, stay dry and exact; the reader supplies the feeling you withheld.

### William F. Buckley Jr. — National Review's mission statement
- **Where:** "Our Mission Statement," National Review, November 19, 1955 (https://www.nationalreview.com/1955/11/our-mission-statement-william-f-buckley-jr/). The founding voice of modern American conservative prose.
- **Voice DNA:** Erudite, witty, and combative at once. He announced that the magazine "stands athwart history, yelling Stop." Long, elegant, Latinate sentences, the opposite of Orwell's plainness, that still land a punch. Proof that high style and hard conviction can live in the same line.
- **Move to steal:** Compress an entire worldview into one vivid, quotable image. "Stands athwart history, yelling Stop" did more for a movement than a thousand position papers.

### Antonin Scalia — the dissents
- **Where:** e.g., the King v. Burwell and Obergefell v. Hodges dissents (both June 2015).
- **Voice DNA:** Legal argument written like a brawl. Scathing, funny, fearless, his coinages ("jiggery-pokery," "pure applesauce," "ask the nearest hippie") make a dense statutory point impossible to forget. The wit is a delivery system for the rigor, never a substitute for it.
- **Move to steal:** Land a technical argument with one outrageous, concrete phrase. A single memorable image carries a dry point further than any careful paragraph.

### Charles Krauthammer — "Things That Matter" / the column
- **Where:** syndicated Washington Post column; collected in *Things That Matter* (2013). Won the 1987 Pulitzer for Commentary.
- **Voice DNA:** Trained as a physician, he diagnoses. Tight, logical, unhurried, building premise by premise until the conclusion feels inevitable. No wasted motion, no throat-clearing. Conviction delivered as clinical clarity.
- **Move to steal:** Build the argument like a proof, one clean claim per paragraph, each earning the next. When the logic is airtight, you never have to raise your voice.

## F. Gonzo / Voice-Forward First Person

The "I" is the whole show. Subjectivity is the point. The story is partly about you failing to cover the story. High style, high risk.

### Hunter S. Thompson — "The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved"
- **Where:** Scanlan's Monthly, June 1970. The birth of Gonzo journalism.
- **Voice DNA:** Sent to cover a horse race, he covers his own boozy disintegration and the grotesque faces in the crowd instead, and that *is* the story. First person as a feature, not a bug. Manic momentum, scene over fact.
- **Move to steal:** When the assigned subject is boring, the real subject is your reaction to it. Turn the camera around, on purpose, with full commitment.

### P.J. O'Rourke — "Parliament of Whores"
- **Where:** 1991 (his book-length tour of the U.S. federal government); also decades of essays in *Rolling Stone* and beyond.
- **Voice DNA:** The libertarian-conservative answer to Thompson, gonzo energy aimed at big government instead of the counterculture. Savage, joke-a-line wit that smuggles in real reporting and a coherent skeptical politics. Funny enough that readers who disagree keep reading.
- **Move to steal:** Make them laugh before they notice they're being argued with. Humor is the Trojan horse for a point your reader came in resisting.

### Joan Didion — "The White Album"
- **Where:** 1979 (collected in *The White Album*); see also "Goodbye to All That," 1967.
- **Voice DNA:** Opens with the most famous line in the canon of personal essay, "We tell ourselves stories in order to live", then dismantles the very idea that the stories hold. Cool, precise, controlled dread. Specific brand names and addresses as talismans against chaos.
- **Move to steal:** State your governing idea as a flat, declarative epigram up front, then spend the piece complicating it. Certainty followed by doubt is more honest than either alone.

### Tom Wolfe — "The Kandy-Kolored Tangerine-Flake Streamline Baby"
- **Where:** Esquire, 1963. A founding document of New Journalism.
- **Voice DNA:** Punctuation as percussion, exclamation points, ellipses, onomatopoeia, italics. He writes the *sound* of custom-car culture, not a report on it. Status detail is his obsession: who's in, who's out, and the exact objects that signal it.
- **Move to steal:** Let the prose rhythm imitate the subject's energy. A frenetic scene can be written frenetically (sparingly, on purpose).

## G. Lyric Essay / Transcendence

Quiet, image-driven, patient. The meaning arrives sideways. Closer to poetry than to journalism. Earns the big feeling by withholding it.

### C.S. Lewis — "The Weight of Glory"
- **Where:** a 1941 sermon, later an essay; see also *Mere Christianity* (1952). Among the most beloved prose for religious and conservative readers, and admired well beyond them.
- **Voice DNA:** Takes the largest possible subjects (longing, glory, the moral law) and makes them plain and warm with homely, concrete analogies, a child making mud pies in a slum because he can't imagine a holiday at sea. Profound ideas in unpretentious words. Never preachy, always inviting.
- **Move to steal:** Render an abstract or spiritual idea through one small, ordinary image. The bigger the concept, the more concrete and humble the picture should be.

### Annie Dillard — "Total Eclipse"
- **Where:** 1982 (collected in *Teaching a Stone to Talk*).
- **Voice DNA:** Describes a solar eclipse as a near-death of the world, building image upon image until the cosmic becomes terrifying and personal. Sensory precision pushed past reporting into the metaphysical. Short paragraphs, long held breath.
- **Move to steal:** Stack concrete sensory images patiently, then let the metaphysical meaning surface on its own. Don't explain the awe; build the conditions for it.

### John Jeremiah Sullivan — "Mister Lytle: An Essay"
- **Where:** The Paris Review, 2010 (collected in *Pulphead*).
- **Voice DNA:** A young writer cares for a dying 92-year-old man of letters; the essay is tender, funny, and ashamed all at once. Confessional honesty about his own motives keeps it from sentimentality. The most human move in the canon: admitting what you got wrong.
- **Move to steal:** Implicate yourself. Admit the unflattering motive (you wanted something from the subject). Self-honesty buys the reader's complete trust.

## K. Bridging the Spectrum / Persuading the Other Side

The hardest, most valuable register: writing that reaches people who don't already agree with you. The enemy here is the tribal applause line, the phrase that makes your own side cheer and slams the door on everyone else. These voices win over skeptics by understanding them first.

### Jonathan Haidt — "The Righteous Mind"
- **Where:** *The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion* (2012); see also "The Coddling of the American Mind" (The Atlantic, 2015, with Greg Lukianoff).
- **Voice DNA:** Explains *why* left and right talk past each other (different moral foundations) and models the fix, he states each side's view in a form its own believers would accept before he critiques it. Calm, curious, evidence-led. The reader never feels attacked, so they keep their guard down.
- **Move to steal:** Steelman before you strike. State the opposing view so fairly that its holders nod, *then* make your case. People only listen to someone who has clearly understood them.

### Peggy Noonan — the Challenger address & the column
- **Where:** Reagan's address after the 1986 Challenger disaster (which she wrote); her WSJ column "Declarations" (since 2000). Won the 2017 Pulitzer for Commentary.
- **Voice DNA:** Plainspoken, emotional, and unifying, prose pitched to the whole country at once, not one faction. The Challenger address closed by borrowing John Gillespie Magee's line about pilots who "slipped the surly bonds of Earth." Her discipline: one or two vivid images, never a clutter of them.
- **Move to steal:** In a moment meant for everyone, strip out the partisan markers and reach for one shared, human image. Restraint, a single clean picture instead of ten, is what lets a line belong to all sides.

### See also: George Orwell (register E)
- Orwell is the patron saint of writing across the divide, a democratic socialist revered by conservatives, because his clarity and honesty outrank his politics. When in doubt, his plainness is the most ecumenical style there is.
