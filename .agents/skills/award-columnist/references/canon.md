# The Canon — close readings of ten pieces of art

Each entry: the piece, the problem it solves, the opening move, the structural shape, the voice fingerprint, and what to steal. Quotations are kept to a phrase or two for craft analysis. To actually read the pieces, follow the citations.

---

## 1. Joan Didion — *Goodbye to All That* (1967)

**Publication:** *Saturday Evening Post*; collected in *Slouching Towards Bethlehem* (Farrar, Straus & Giroux, 1968).
**Problem solved:** The farewell-to-a-place essay without nostalgia or sentiment. The essay every younger writer eventually tries to imitate when leaving New York, San Francisco, London — and usually fails.

**Opening move.** She begins with a single line that admits the trick of the whole essay: it is easy to see where things begin, harder to see where they end. The sentence sounds offhand but it is a thesis disguised as a remark. The reader is already inside the argument.

**Structural shape.** Time-loops. The essay moves between three Didions: the twenty-year-old arriving on a TWA flight, the twenty-eight-year-old who has stopped going to parties, and the narrator writing from California with the clarity of distance. None of these Didions is shown sentimentally. The structure is *not* chronological — it is *associative*, moving by mood and image. The reader feels the wash of remembered time, not a timeline.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Cool, observational.* She does not perform emotion; she describes the conditions under which she felt it
- *Specific names, specific addresses.* The Hotel Pierre. Lexington Avenue. A particular smell. The specificity grounds the abstraction
- *Long, looping sentences alternated with very short ones.* A 60-word sentence about Idlewild airport followed by a four-word verdict
- *The signature move: stating an emotion by stating its absence.* "I was no longer afraid." She tells you what changed by naming what stopped
- *No moralizing.* She refuses to tell you what to think about the New York she's describing. The judgment is in the selection

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *"of course"* deployment. She uses *of course* not to signal a known truth but to confide in the reader — *of course this happened, you would have seen it coming, we are both adults*. It creates instant intimacy without earning it sentimentally.

**Structural move to steal.** End where you began, but the same image now means something different. The essay closes on a loop back to the opening — the same arrival, reread by someone older.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Open with a sentence that sounds incidental but is the thesis
- Refuse to perform the emotion you want the reader to feel
- Name the street, the brand, the year, the smell
- Close by looping back to the opening image, changed

## 2. Hunter S. Thompson — *The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved* (1970)

**Publication:** *Scanlan's Monthly*, June 1970. Generally credited as the founding text of gonzo journalism.
**Problem solved:** What if the assignment is rotten — covering a famous event that has become a parade of cliché — and the only honest move is to make *yourself* the subject? Thompson invents the answer in real time.

**Opening move.** He doesn't begin at the Derby. He begins on a plane next to a Texan who wants to talk about the Black Panthers. The opening is misdirection — it tells you the piece will *not* be about horses, it will be about the people who go to see horses. The voice is established before the assignment is even mentioned.

**Structural shape.** Three movements. (1) Arrival and infiltration: the Louisville hotel, the swelling crowd, Thompson's accomplice the illustrator Ralph Steadman, the deliberate provocations. (2) The Derby itself, mostly off-page — Thompson barely watches the race. (3) The closing scene of recognition: Thompson sees a grotesque, hungover face in a mirror, and realizes the face is his.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Paranoid baroque.* The world is conspiring; the reporter is barely holding it together
- *Catalogues of grotesques.* He names what he sees in lists — faces, hats, drinks, costumes — and the listing itself becomes indictment
- *Reported speech in italics and exclamations.* Other people sound louder than the narrator
- *The reporter as the subject's moral instrument.* The piece is not "about" the Derby; the piece is *what the Derby did to me, and what that says about the Derby*
- *Drug-saturated paranoia presented as observational realism.* The reader is never told whether the narrator is reliable, and the unreliability is the point

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *physical-detail accusation*. Instead of saying *the crowd was crass*, he writes a one-sentence portrait of a drunk man's face — and the reader supplies the judgment. He never tells; the catalogue tells.

**Structural move to steal.** Bury the lede. The expected subject (the race) is barely present. The real subject (the crowd, the city, the writer) is everywhere. If your assigned topic is boring, write around it until the *real* topic reveals itself.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- If the assignment is rotten, write the *meta-piece* about why
- Catalogue grotesques rather than judge them
- Let the reporter's deterioration be the moral argument
- End on a moment of self-recognition rather than a thesis statement

## 3. Gay Talese — *Frank Sinatra Has a Cold* (1966)

**Publication:** *Esquire*, April 1966. Routinely cited as the greatest magazine article ever written. Founding text of New Journalism.
**Problem solved:** How do you write a profile of a subject who refuses to talk to you?

**Backstory worth knowing.** Talese arrived in Los Angeles to interview Sinatra. Sinatra had a cold and would not see him. Talese stayed for three months. He interviewed Sinatra's bodyguard, his valet, his hairdresser, his daughter, the people who were in the room when Sinatra walked through it. The result is a portrait of Sinatra constructed entirely from the periphery — which turns out to be a more revealing portrait than any direct interview would have produced.

**Opening move.** A scene in a Beverly Hills bar. Sinatra is at the corner of the bar with two blonde women, not talking, smoking, wearing a snap-brim hat. A pool player named Harlan Ellison walks in wearing boots Sinatra doesn't like. The confrontation that follows is small — a question, a remark, a flick of impatience — but the entire piece is set up by it. We learn the kind of man Sinatra is by watching how the room arranges itself around him.

**Structural shape.** A novel in miniature. The piece moves through scenes the way a film does — the bar, the recording studio, the boxing match, the daughter's house, the airport. Each scene is reconstructed with cinematic detail: who sat where, what they wore, what was said, what the temperature was. Between scenes, Talese inserts essayistic passages — Sinatra's place in American masculinity, his hold on his employees, the meaning of the cold.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Novelistic close-reading.* The prose reads like fiction; the facts are reported. This is the New Journalism move
- *The periphery as the subject's reflection.* We meet Sinatra through the bodyguard's posture, the valet's anxiety, the daughter's loyalty
- *Specific physical detail.* The toupee. The orange necktie. The exact age of Sinatra's mother. The brand of bourbon
- *Sentences that build through subordinate clauses without losing the spine.* Talese can run a sentence to 70 words and the reader never gets lost because the verb is anchored early
- *No first person.* Talese is invisible in the piece. He never says *I*. The result is a piece that feels *seen* rather than told

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *cataloguing sentence as portrait*. Talese lists the men around Sinatra — name, age, role, salary, year hired — and the list itself becomes a portrait of Sinatra's court. Pure information, voiced.

**Structural move to steal.** *Refraction.* If you cannot get the subject, get everyone who surrounds the subject. The portrait built from periphery is often more honest than the portrait built from the interview.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Open with a small scene that contains the whole argument
- Refract the subject through the people in the room
- Use the cold (the small, dismissible thing) as the symbol for the larger condition
- Report fiction-grade detail and let the reader supply the judgment

## 4. Tom Wolfe — *The Last American Hero Is Junior Johnson. Yes!* (1965)

**Publication:** *Esquire*, March 1965. Collected in *The Kandy-Kolored Tangerine-Flake Streamline Baby*.
**Problem solved:** How do you write about a subculture (Southern stock-car racing, in this case) without condescending to it and without going native? Wolfe's answer: take it more seriously than it takes itself, and use prose that *sounds like* the subculture's noise.

**Opening move.** A wall of noise. Wolfe opens with the literal sound of stock cars and the literal sound of a Southern crowd, transcribed onto the page with italics, repetitions, ellipses, and exclamations. The first paragraph is performance — the reader is *placed* at North Wilkesboro Speedway before any argument begins.

**Structural shape.** The piece moves in three layers: (1) the world of stock-car racing as a sensory event, (2) Junior Johnson the man and his biography (the moonshine-running Appalachian backstory), (3) Wolfe's argument that Junior Johnson is the genuine American folk hero of the moment and that the people writing for *Esquire's* audience have failed to notice. Each layer reinforces the others; the piece does not feel "structured" because the noise carries it forward.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Maximalist.* Sentences that pile on detail until they break and then keep going
- *Sound effects on the page.* *Vroom. Eeeee-ooooo.* Italics for inflection. ALL CAPS for shouting
- *Punctuation as music.* Ellipses. Em-dashes. Exclamation points used unironically
- *Anthropological vocabulary in service of respect, not condescension.* Wolfe names the rituals (the way crowds dress, the way drivers walk) with the precision of someone who has been watching, and the precision communicates respect
- *Direct address to the reader.* "Ten o'clock Sunday morning…" The voice grabs the reader by the lapel

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *sensory comma cascade*. Wolfe will string sensory details with commas — color, sound, smell, feel of the air — for half a paragraph, then stop short with a four-word verdict. The cadence is intentional: pile up the world, then name it.

**Structural move to steal.** *Respect through specificity.* If you are writing about a subculture you do not belong to, the way to avoid condescension is to learn the vocabulary, the rituals, the names — and use them without translation. The respect is in the precision.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Open with sensory immersion before any argument
- Use punctuation as music — italics, em-dashes, exclamation points (sparingly, for effect)
- Catalogue the rituals of the subculture you're describing
- Name the people, the cars, the brands, the years; do not generalize

## 5. Christopher Hitchens — *Why Women Aren't Funny* (2007)

**Publication:** *Vanity Fair*, January 2007.
**Problem solved:** How do you write a provocation that infuriates half your readers, makes the other half laugh, and gets quoted for fifteen years — without sounding like a crank? Hitchens's answer: calibrate the provocation, then over-deliver on the prose.

**Opening move.** He concedes the absurdity of the thesis in the first paragraph. The piece begins by acknowledging that the reader will dismiss it, then doubles down. The opening is a dare to keep reading. It is also a feint — the eventual argument is more anthropological than the title suggests.

**Structural shape.** Five movements. (1) The provocation stated. (2) The evolutionary case (women select for humor in men, men do not need to select for humor in women). (3) The literary case (Dorothy Parker is the exception that proves something). (4) The list of named exceptions and what they share. (5) The kicker — a turn that walks back the headline just enough to expose what the piece was actually about.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Long sentences with the verb early.* Hitchens can run a 50-word sentence with three subordinate clauses and the reader never loses the spine because the main verb arrived in word eight
- *Literary references casual but exact.* Kingsley Amis, Nietzsche, the Old Testament — never with citation, never showing off, always doing argumentative work
- *Contempt earned, never performed.* When he turns on someone, the prose stays mannered. The mannered surface makes the contempt land harder
- *The reader treated as an equal.* He assumes you have read the books. He assumes you can follow the joke. The flattery of being assumed competent is part of why readers stayed loyal
- *Mock-judicial register.* "It is widely understood that…" He sounds like a brief; the formality is itself comic

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *Latinate concession*. A Latinate sentence acknowledging the opposing view, immediately undercut by an Anglo-Saxon hammer. Long–short. Fancy–plain. He sets up a position with elegance and then knocks it down with a monosyllable. The contrast is the joke and the argument at once.

**Structural move to steal.** The *headline-as-trojan-horse*. The title is the most inflammatory version of the thesis. The piece itself argues a more nuanced version of the same idea. The reader who came in furious finds themselves agreeing by paragraph six, which is the moment Hitchens has them.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Concede the provocation in the first paragraph so the rest of the piece has room to maneuver
- Make literary references that *do* argumentative work; cut the ones that show off
- Set up positions with Latinate elegance, knock them down with Anglo-Saxon hammers
- Land the kicker on a turn, not a restatement

## 6. David Foster Wallace — *Consider the Lobster* (2004)

**Publication:** *Gourmet*, August 2004.
**Problem solved:** You are sent to cover a food festival. You return having written a moral inquiry into the ethics of boiling a sentient creature alive. How do you turn a five-paragraph assignment into a piece readers still teach in MFA programs?

**Backstory worth knowing.** *Gourmet* sent Wallace to the Maine Lobster Festival expecting a colorful magazine feature about a regional food celebration. He delivered a long, footnoted essay asking whether it is ethical to eat lobster at all. The magazine published it anyway, more or less intact. The editorial decision is part of the piece's legend.

**Opening move.** A flat, deadpan description of what the Maine Lobster Festival is — dates, attendance, parking, ticket prices — written in the register of a slightly bored encyclopedia entry. The boredom is a setup. The reader thinks: *oh, a food festival piece*. By page two, the piece has turned and is asking whether the lobster feels pain.

**Structural shape.** A spiral. The piece begins as travel writing, slips into culinary history, slips into neurology, slips into moral philosophy, and never explicitly announces the genre shifts. The footnotes carry an enormous percentage of the argument — they are not asides, they are a parallel essay running underneath the main text. The main text gives you the festival; the footnotes give you the questions about the festival the main text cannot quite ask without breaking format.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Hyperliterate honesty.* Wallace will not let himself off the hook. Every position is qualified by the question of whether he holds it for good reasons
- *Footnotes that argue.* Not citations, not asides — full essays-within-the-essay. A footnote can run 200 words and contain the moral crux of the piece
- *Parentheticals stacked three deep.* (And the parentheticals are doing the same work the footnotes are: qualifying, doubting, adding evidence the main sentence cannot hold.)
- *Vocabulary range from academic to vernacular within a single paragraph.* He will use *qualia* and *real real* in the same passage. The contrast is the voice
- *Refusal to land on a moral verdict.* The piece asks the question and trusts the reader to sit with it. The discomfort is the point

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *self-correcting parenthetical*. He writes a confident sentence, then opens a parenthetical that doubts it. The reader sees the writer thinking in real time. The result feels honest in a way a polished sentence cannot.

**Structural move to steal.** The *quiet genre shift*. Start in the register the assignment seemed to call for. Drift toward the larger question without announcing the drift. By the time the reader notices, they are inside the moral question, and they cannot leave.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Begin in the expected register; drift toward the real subject
- Use footnotes (or extended parentheticals) to carry the doubts the main text cannot hold
- Mix vocabulary registers within a paragraph for friction
- Refuse the easy verdict; trust the reader

## 7. Ta-Nehisi Coates — *The Case for Reparations* (2014)

**Publication:** *The Atlantic*, June 2014. Often cited as the most influential magazine article of its decade.
**Problem solved:** How do you persuade a skeptical reader of a thesis they have spent their adult life dismissing? Coates's answer: do not argue. Build.

**Opening move.** A single person — Clyde Ross, a Black homeowner in Chicago — and the contract that swindled him out of his house. The piece begins not with the thesis but with a man, an address, a year, and a document. The argument is built underneath the man's life; by the time the thesis is stated, the reader has already felt the case.

**Structural shape.** Ten sections, each headed by a Roman numeral and a one-line epigraph. The sections do not argue toward the thesis in a linear way. They *accumulate*. Section I is Clyde Ross. Section II is the history of contract sales in Chicago. Section III steps further back to the Black Codes. Section IV further back to slavery. The piece moves in widening historical circles, each one anchored in specific people and specific documents. The thesis — that the United States owes a debt that must be reckoned with — becomes inescapable not because Coates argues it but because the weight of accumulated evidence makes any other conclusion feel evasive.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Cumulative force.* Each paragraph carries one specific fact, one specific person, one specific document. The argument is the pile
- *No rhetorical pyrotechnics.* The prose is plain, even austere. Coates trusts the material
- *Historical specificity as moral force.* He cites the year, the bank, the contract, the address. The specificity is not academic; it is *prosecutorial*
- *Personal restraint.* The reader knows Coates is angry, but the anger is held back; it shows through the curation of evidence, not the adjectives
- *The named human in every section.* Even the most archival sections are anchored to a named person with an address. Abstractions are kept short, then immediately grounded

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *single declarative paragraph*. A one-sentence paragraph, plainly stated, after a long passage of historical evidence. It functions like a judge's ruling at the end of a brief. The white space around it does the rhetorical work.

**Structural move to steal.** *The widening circle*. Start with one person and one moment. Move outward in time. Each move outward earns its scale because the previous section has done the close-up work first. By the time the piece reaches the scale of national history, the reader is willing to follow.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Anchor every section in a named, specific person
- Let the evidence accumulate without commentary; trust the pile
- Widening circles: small moment, then the history that produced it, then the history that produced *that*
- A one-sentence paragraph after a long passage hits like a verdict

## 8. Matt Levine — *Money Stuff* (Bloomberg, 2013–present; precursor at Dealbreaker 2011–13)

**Representative pieces.** The JPMorgan "London Whale" columns of 2012–13 (originally at Dealbreaker; cited in CJR's *Best Business Writing 2013*) are the foundational set. The current *Money Stuff* newsletter (Bloomberg Opinion) is the mature form.
**Problem solved:** How do you explain finance — which is technical, baroque, and frequently absurd — to a general reader without dumbing it down *or* losing the absurdity? Levine's answer: write it straight, and let the parentheticals carry the wink.

**Opening move.** A Levine column typically opens with a one-sentence framing of an event in finance — usually a recent news item — written in a faux-neutral register, immediately followed by a parenthetical that contains the joke. The opening establishes the contract: the surface is explanatory, the depths are sardonic.

**Structural shape.** Loose. A typical *Money Stuff* moves through three or four news items in a single newsletter, each treated as a worked example of a deeper principle (regulatory arbitrage, market microstructure, executive compensation, securities fraud). The pieces do not "build" the way an essay builds; they accumulate worked examples until the reader has internalized a way of seeing. The column is, in effect, a long-running tutorial on financial absurdity.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Parentheticals carry half the argument.* The main sentence reports; the parenthetical interprets, doubts, undercuts, jokes. The voice lives in the parenthetical
- *Dry irony.* He almost never raises his voice. The flatter the delivery, the funnier the absurdity beneath
- *Recurring phrases as voice signatures.* "But anyway," "I guess," "I mean, sure," "you can see why," "no, wait." These are not tics; they are voice-anchors. Used consistently, they become a fingerprint readers recognize immediately
- *Plain English for technical content.* Where another writer would jargon up, Levine de-jargons. "Bond" not "fixed-income instrument." "Loan" not "credit facility." The simplicity makes the absurdity legible
- *The repeated thesis joke.* He has running gags ("everything is securities fraud," "everything is private equity") that compress entire arguments into a phrase

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *deadpan plus parenthetical*. State the absurd thing flatly. Open a parenthetical. Undercut it. Close. The reader feels superior — they got the joke without being told it was a joke — and the writer feels intimate.

**Structural move to steal.** The *worked-example column*. Instead of arguing a thesis abstractly, parade three news items through the same analytical lens. By item three, the reader has the framework. The framework is the piece.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Open with a flat factual sentence, then a parenthetical that does the interpretive work
- Build voice through one or two recurring phrases used over multiple pieces
- Use the simplest possible word for technical content; let the absurdity show through the plain language
- Trust the reader to get the joke

## 9. George Orwell — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Publication:** *Horizon*, April 1946. The essay every essayist eventually reads and reads again.
**Problem solved:** Bad political prose makes bad political thought possible. How do you write about *writing* in a way that is itself a demonstration of the cure?

**Opening move.** A flat statement that the English language is in a bad way, followed by the concession that most people regard this as a natural process beyond control. Orwell then disputes that concession in plain English. The opening is itself a worked example: he names the position he will oppose, in the prose he will recommend.

**Structural shape.** Six movements. (1) The diagnosis — modern English prose is decadent. (2) Five exhibits — actual passages of bad writing, dissected line by line. (3) Catalogue of vices: dying metaphors, operators (false verbs), pretentious diction, meaningless words. (4) A worked example: a Biblical passage rewritten in modern bureaucratic English to show what is lost. (5) Six rules. (6) A larger argument about politics and clarity.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Plain Anglo-Saxon English.* Short words first, abstractions earned
- *Specific exhibits.* He does not say "much modern prose is bad" — he quotes five paragraphs, names the authors, and pulls them apart
- *Mock-bureaucratic translation as comic device.* The rewritten Ecclesiastes passage is the most-quoted gag in the essay — a deliberate translation of beautiful prose into ugly prose, to make the cost visible
- *Rules at the end, not at the beginning.* He earns the right to issue rules by spending the essay showing why each rule is necessary
- *Quiet moral seriousness.* The argument is finally that bad prose enables political evasion. He never raises his voice when making it

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *exhibit-and-dissect*. Don't tell the reader the prose is bad. Quote it. Then walk through what is broken. The specificity is what makes the critique stick.

**Structural move to steal.** *Earn the rules.* If you are going to issue prescriptions, spend the body of the piece showing the disease. The rules at the end then feel inevitable rather than imposed.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Use the shortest word that works
- Quote your enemies in full; do not paraphrase them into easier targets
- Use mock-translation as a way to show what register costs
- Issue rules at the end, after you have earned them

## 10. Tom Junod — *The Falling Man* (2003)

**Publication:** *Esquire*, September 2003.
**Problem solved:** How do you write about an image — the AP photograph of a man falling from the World Trade Center on September 11 — that the public has refused to look at, without exploiting the image or the man?

**Opening move.** A description of the photograph itself. Quietly. The lines, the geometry, the man's posture, the building behind him. Junod writes the photograph as a composition before he writes it as a death. The opening forces the reader to look at what they have refused to look at, and the looking is done with reverence rather than spectacle.

**Structural shape.** Three braided threads. (1) The photograph and the photographer (Richard Drew). (2) The reception of the photograph — its appearance in newspapers on September 12, the immediate backlash, its subsequent disappearance from print. (3) The search for the man's identity — a careful, partial, ultimately unresolved attempt to name him. The braid alternates: composition, reception, search.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Restrained reverence.* The prose is quiet. Where another writer would reach for adjectives, Junod chooses one and trusts it
- *Voice as what the writer refuses to say.* He does not editorialize about the politics of the image. He describes the family of one possible identification and lets the description carry the weight
- *Sentences that breathe.* Short. Then short. Then a longer one that opens onto a small specific detail. The cadence respects the silence the subject deserves
- *Refusal of consolation.* The piece does not resolve the man's identity. The refusal is itself a moral position — the reader is not given the closure of a name
- *Second-person used sparingly and exactly.* When Junod addresses the reader directly, it is to implicate them in the looking

**Sentence-level move to steal.** The *adjective held back*. Where the temptation is to reach for *unbearable, horrifying, tragic*, Junod chooses one mild adjective and trusts it. The under-modification creates the emotional pressure the over-modification would have released.

**Structural move to steal.** The *braided thread*. Three parallel narratives, alternating section by section, each picking up where the last left off. The reader carries all three at once; the meaning emerges from their proximity.

**What to steal for a generic piece:**
- Where the subject is heavy, under-modify. Let the noun do the work
- Refuse the consolation of resolution if the truth is unresolved
- Address the reader directly only when you mean to implicate them
- Sentences should breathe — short, short, longer, short

## How to use the canon

When a writing task comes in, ask first: *which of the ten pieces solves the closest problem?*

- Memoir-essay, farewell to a place or phase → **Didion**
- Event reportage where the event is hollow and the writer is the story → **Thompson**
- Profile, especially of someone who won't talk → **Talese**
- Subculture or scene that mainstream readers don't take seriously → **Wolfe**
- Contrarian provocation, takedown, argument-as-combat → **Hitchens**
- Small assignment becoming a moral inquiry → **Wallace**
- Long argument that needs to overcome skepticism through accumulation → **Coates**
- Business / finance / how-the-plumbing-works → **Levine**
- A piece about language, politics, or the cost of bad writing → **Orwell**
- Grief, scale, the unspeakable; an image or event everyone is refusing to look at → **Junod**

Often the right answer is a blend (Talese's scene-construction with Coates's accumulating evidence; Wallace's footnoted doubt with Hitchens's literary register; Junod's restraint with Didion's time-loops). Name the blend in the trace.

When in doubt, reread the relevant entry above before drafting. Five minutes of reading a canon entry produces a draft of recognizably better composition.
