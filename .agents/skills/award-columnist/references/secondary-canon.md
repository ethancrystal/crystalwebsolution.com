# Secondary canon — voices beyond the core ten

The core ten cover most of what an essayist needs. But some registers and traditions live outside that ten and deserve their own pages — Indian business commentary, the UK column tradition, narrative-feature heavyweights the core ten didn't include, and the viral/social register that powers modern web traffic.

Reach for this file when the topic, audience, or required register doesn't match any of the core ten.

---

## Indian business and policy commentary

The Indian English-language press has its own column tradition — sharp, often contrarian, willing to be funny in ways American business writing rarely is, and frequently more rigorous on macro than the equivalent American column. Three names worth studying.

### Mihir S. Sharma — Bloomberg Opinion / Business Standard

**Where to read.** Bloomberg Opinion (current); Business Standard editorial board (long-running). Author of *Restart: The Last Chance for the Indian Economy* (2015), which won the Tata LitLive Business Book of the Year.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Wit deployed against complacency.* He treats Indian policy debates as deserving the same scrutiny anyone would apply to a developed-country government — which is itself a contrarian position
- *Short paragraphs, long arguments.* Each paragraph one move; the case builds across many small steps
- *Specific numbers as moral weight.* He cites the actual fiscal deficit, the actual GST collection, the actual employment rate. Numbers as accountability
- *Comparative framing.* India compared to its own past, to its peers, to what it claims to be — never to a fantasy version of itself

**What to steal.** When writing about policy, refuse the comforting frame. Use the numbers the official communications hope you will not quote.

### Andy Mukherjee — Bloomberg Opinion

**Where to read.** Bloomberg Opinion, focusing on Asian finance and corporate governance.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Data-with-attitude.* The column reads like Matt Levine relocated to Singapore — financial absurdity treated with dry irony, but rooted in Asian markets and Indian corporate governance
- *The technical detail that locks in the verdict.* He explains the accounting move and then names what it actually is
- *Polite ruthlessness.* The prose is mannered. The verdicts are merciless

**What to steal.** When writing about corporate behavior, do not announce the verdict. Walk the reader through the mechanism. The reader arrives at the verdict on their own and feels smarter for it.

### T. N. Ninan — Business Standard / *The Turn of the Tortoise*

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Long-arc historical framing.* Treats each week's news as a chapter in a multi-decade story about Indian capitalism
- *Personal authority earned over decades.* The voice does not perform expertise; it assumes it
- *Restraint as a position.* Where the column could go for the throat, he chooses understatement. The understatement is itself the rhetorical weight

**What to steal.** When you have actually been watching a beat for a long time, do not show off the watching. Trust that the reader can tell from the framing. The deeper the well, the quieter the voice.

## The UK column tradition

UK columnists generally write shorter than American magazine writers, with denser per-paragraph wit and less reverence. Three contemporary names plus the historical heavyweight.

### Caitlin Moran — *The Times* (UK)

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *The confidential register.* She writes as if the reader is the friend she is telling the story to over a kitchen table. The intimacy is the rhetorical engine
- *Self-deprecation as setup, not deflection.* She admits to the small embarrassment to earn the right to make the larger argument
- *Specific cultural reference points, mid-sentence.* Music, TV, books, foods, brands — used as shorthand. The shorthand presumes the reader is in the same cultural conversation
- *Italics for inflection.* Words italicized the way they would be stressed in conversation

**What to steal.** When writing about personal-cultural subjects, drop the journalistic distance. Write the way you would actually talk to someone you like. The confidential register is unfakeable — it has to come from real specificity, not from styled informality.

### Hugo Young — *The Guardian* (1929–2003)

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Gravitas earned by clarity.* The sentences are simple; the analysis is not. The reader trusts the voice because the voice trusts the reader
- *Historical memory as authority.* He cites the precedent — the previous government's analogous decision, the parallel from forty years ago — without dating himself
- *Refusal of partisanship as a discipline, not a posture.* He criticizes from all sides. The criticism is earned because the consistency is real

**What to steal.** When writing political commentary, the position you take is less important than the consistency with which you apply your own standards. Voice is the cumulative trust the reader builds across pieces.

### A. A. Gill — *The Sunday Times* (1954–2016)

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *The exuberant takedown.* Reviews of restaurants and TV that became literary events. The form ate itself; what remained was the voice
- *Sentences that go further than they should.* A clause that adds one more image after the sentence has finished its work. The over-reach is the move
- *Cultural reference as native vocabulary.* Not showing off — assuming
- *Mercy used selectively.* When he praised, the praise had weight because the takedowns had weight first

**What to steal.** If you intend to be merciless, be merciless about specifics, not generalities. The takedown of a *named dish* is criticism; the takedown of "modern cuisine" is venting.

## US heavyweights not in the core ten

A few writers whose work belongs in any extended canon.

### Susan Orlean — *The New Yorker*

**Representative piece.** "The American Man, Age Ten" (*Esquire*, 1992). Also "Orchid Fever" / *The Orchid Thief* (1998).

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Curiosity as the engine.* She is not arguing a thesis; she is following an interest the reader did not know they had
- *The subject treated as worthy of full attention regardless of its scale.* A ten-year-old boy. An orchid collector. The attention is the dignity
- *Sentences that catch the rhythm of the subject.* Writing about a kid, the sentences shorten and run on like a kid's monologue. Writing about a quiet eccentric, the sentences slow and observe

**What to steal.** For profiles, find the question the subject does not realize they are answering. Then build the piece around that question.

### Michael Lewis — *Vanity Fair*, *Bloomberg*, books

**Representative pieces.** *Liar's Poker* (1989). *The Big Short* (2010). "The End" (*Portfolio*, 2008) — the precursor to *The Big Short*.

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Character as the engine of business writing.* He does not explain the subprime crisis through mechanism first. He explains it through Steve Eisman, Michael Burry, the unlikely outsiders who saw it
- *Comedy as moral instrument.* Where the situation is absurd, he writes it as comedy. The comedy is the ethical position
- *Plain English for technical content.* He explains a credit default swap by explaining what the people trading them were thinking about

**What to steal.** When writing about a system, find the outsider who saw what the insiders missed. The piece is the outsider; the system is the backdrop.

### Janet Malcolm — *The New Yorker*

**Voice fingerprint.**
- *Surgical sentences.* Each one feels rewritten until it could not be rewritten further
- *The narrator as analyst, not participant.* She is in the room but writing from outside it
- *The famous opening of "The Journalist and the Murderer."* That every journalist who is not too stupid or too full of himself to notice what is going on knows that what he does is morally indefensible. (One of the most quoted sentences in journalism criticism. Worth studying not for the politics but for the *length and architecture* of the sentence — how it holds a long argument together without losing the spine)

**What to steal.** When you have a hard thing to say, the sentence has to be long enough to do the work and tight enough that the reader does not lose the spine. Rewrite the sentence ten times until it is the one sentence.

## The viral / social register

A different register entirely — the voice of viral web copy, the Lad Bible / Buzzfeed / TikTok-caption tradition. Different audience, different attention budget, different conventions.

This register is often dismissed by literary writers, which is a mistake. At its sharpest, it does a real job — translating a story into the cadence of attention that exists on the modern feed. The same craft principles apply (verbs do the work, specifics anchor, kicker lands), but the conventions are different.

**Conventions of the viral register:**

- *Very short paragraphs.* Often one sentence each. White space is the dominant design element
- *Second person, frequent direct address.* "You're not going to believe…" / "If you've ever…"
- *The conspiratorial intimacy.* The voice assumes the reader is already an insider. The piece does not explain context; it presumes shared knowledge
- *The hook in the first six words.* Not the lede sentence — the lede *phrase*. The first six words have to earn the next six
- *Numbered or bulleted structure even when the content would not require it.* The format is itself a contract: *this will be quick, this will be scannable, you will not have to commit*
- *Repetition for rhythm.* Echoing a phrase later in the piece for the click-shut feeling
- *The reveal in the kicker.* The piece is structured around a delayed payoff. The reader scrolls because something is being held back

**What to steal — for non-viral pieces.** The brevity discipline. Forcing the first six words to carry the next six is a habit that improves any opening, in any register. The white-space discipline (one-sentence paragraphs used deliberately, not constantly) gives short writing rhythm.

**What not to import.** Conspiratorial intimacy without earning it; assumed shared knowledge with a reader who does not in fact share it; bullets where prose would do; manufactured surprise.

The viral register is genuinely good at one thing — keeping the reader on the page. Steal that discipline. Leave the cliches behind.

## Using this file

When the piece in front of you doesn't match any of the core ten cleanly, scan this file for a voice that does.

- Indian business or policy piece → Sharma, Mukherjee, or Ninan
- UK column or feature → Moran, Young, or Gill
- Long magazine feature with a curious-but-not-confrontational voice → Orlean
- Long-form business narrative with characters as the engine → Lewis
- The single hardest sentence has to be perfect → Malcolm
- Content that has to perform on the modern feed → the viral conventions (with discipline)

Often the right answer is a *blend* across both files: a Levine-style finance core in a Mukherjee setting, Orlean's curiosity arc with Junod's restraint, Moran's confidential register with Hitchens's argumentative spine. Name the blend in the trace.
