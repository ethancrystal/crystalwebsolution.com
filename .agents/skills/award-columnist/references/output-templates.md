# Output templates — formats for different venues

The skill produces prose. The prose still has to fit the venue. Below are six output formats and the template for each. When a piece is requested, identify the venue first; pick the template; then apply the canon craft *inside* the template.

The templates are skeletons — they specify structure and sectioning. The voice, sentence-level craft, opening moves, and kicker come from the main protocol and the canon. Never reach for a template before doing Phase 1–3 (Inventory, Thesis, Voice).

---

## Template 1 — The SEO pillar article (long-form business / service blog)

**Use when:** the user asks for a "complete guide to X," "pillar article," "long-form blog post," "ultimate guide," "comprehensive guide," or asks for SEO-optimized content aimed at ranking for a category term. Typical length 2,500–4,500 words. Typical purpose: rank for a high-intent keyword AND convert.

**Use the canon voice inside, not outside.** The pillar format has a fixed structure (FAQ at the bottom, comparison section in the middle, etc.) — but the prose inside each section should still hit Phase 6 craft standards. Specific scenarios in the opening. Strong verbs. Plain English. No "delve." No "ever-evolving landscape." The structure is the SEO; the prose is the craft.

### Structure

```
# [Title]: [Subtitle with year and category modifiers]

> **Meta Title:** [55–60 chars including year and primary keyword]
> **Meta Description:** [150–160 chars summarizing value + soft CTA]
> **Target Keywords:** [primary keyword], [secondary keyword 1], [secondary keyword 2]...
> **URL Slug:** /blog/[hyphenated-slug-with-primary-keyword]

---

[OPENING — three or four short paragraphs.
- Paragraph 1: three or four specific scenarios in short sentences. ("Someone owes you money. A contractor walked away. Your landlord is sitting on your security deposit.") Each scenario is a real situation the reader recognizes.
- Paragraph 2: name the question. ("You know you're in the right — but what do you actually do about it?")
- Paragraph 3: state the answer in one sentence. ("For most people, the answer starts with [X].")
- Paragraph 4: one credibility paragraph with a real stat from a recognizable source (ABA, Gartner, McKinsey, government data). Frame the stat as proof that the rest of the article is worth the read.
- Paragraph 5: one-line table-of-contents preview. ("This guide covers everything you need to know: what X is, when you need one, what it should include, how much it costs, and how to get one in minutes.")]

---

## What Is [X]?

[Two or three paragraphs.
- Paragraph 1: definition in plain English. Use a metaphor or comparison the reader already understands. ("Think of it as the official 'I'm serious' notice before legal action.")
- Paragraph 2: name the three (or four) functions it serves. Use a numbered or "First...Second...Third" prose construction, not bullets. Each function is one or two sentences.
- Paragraph 3: define by what it is NOT. ("X is not Y. It's not Z. It is W.") The negative definition is more memorable than the positive one.]

---

## When Do You Need [X]?

[Two paragraphs of intro + six to eight scenario sections.

Intro: "X applies to a surprisingly wide range of situations. If [condition], a [thing] is almost always the right next step."

Then six to eight bold-led paragraphs, each one a scenario:

**[Scenario name].** One or two sentences describing the situation. End with what the reader does about it ("Demand letters are the standard first step for debt recovery across all 50 states.")

Each scenario is a different reader's problem. Cover the full range — personal, business, edge cases. The reader should see themselves in at least one of these.

Closing paragraph: name the common thread across the scenarios. ("The common thread across all of these: you have a legitimate claim, the other party isn't responding to informal communication, and you want to resolve it without immediately jumping to expensive litigation.")]

---

## What Should [X] Include?

[Intro paragraph: state that "an effective X isn't just [the obvious thing]. It follows a specific structure that [the relevant authority] recognizes and [the recipient/audience] takes seriously."

Then six to eight bold-led paragraphs, each one a required element:

**[Element name].** One sentence saying what it is. One sentence with an actual concrete example in quotes. ("Be precise. 'I demand payment of $8,500 within 14 days' is effective. 'I want my money back' is not.")

The example sentences in quotes are the single most important compositional move in this section. They demonstrate the standard you are describing. They are also the most memorable thing in the article.]

---

## How Much Does [X] Cost?

[The cost section is structured as comparison. Three or four cost tiers, each one bold-led:

**[Option 1: traditional].** Cost range. Why people use it. Why the cost is high.

**[Option 2: middle ground].** Cost range. The tradeoff.

**[Option 3: DIY / template].** Cost (often free). Why it backfires.

**[Your product / service]:** the value proposition stated as a direct comparison. Specific price. Specific feature. Specific savings percentage. End with an inline CTA.

[**Get your [X] now →**](https://[your-site]/signup)

The CTA is *inline*, mid-article, after the price comparison. Not at the end. The reader who has just been told the comparison is the reader most likely to click.]

---

## [X] vs. [Related Thing]: What's the Difference?

[The disambiguation section. Two or three paragraphs comparing the requested thing to a related thing the reader might be confusing it with. Close with: "Sometimes the right answer is both."

This section captures search traffic from people who don't yet know which one they need. It also pre-empts the most common objection ("I think I need Y, not X").]

---

## Do [X] Actually Work?

[The proof section. Open with "Yes — and the data backs it up." Then:

- Paragraph 1: a real statistic with a real source.
- Paragraph 2: explain WHY they work. Name the three reasons the recipient takes them seriously.
- Paragraph 3: name the conditions under which they DON'T work. (Honesty about failure modes is the move that buys trust. The reader believes the wins because you admitted the losses.)]

---

## How to [Do the Adjacent Action — Send / Use / File X]

[The how-to section. Three or four bold-led options, ordered from strongest to weakest:

**[Method 1].** Cost. Why it's the gold standard.

**[Method 2].** Cost. When to use it.

**[Method 3].** Cost. The edge case.

Close with one line of what NOT to do. ("Never send a demand letter via text message.")]

---

## How Long Should You Wait After [the action]?

[Process / timeline section. Open with the standard expectation. Then four options for what to do next, each bold-led:

**[Option 1: follow-up].** When to use it.

**[Option 2: escalate path A].** When it's appropriate.

**[Option 3: escalate path B].** When it's appropriate.

**[Option 4: regulatory].** The nuclear option.]

---

## State-by-State / Jurisdiction Considerations

[The "why this matters for YOUR situation" section. Three paragraphs.

- Paragraph 1: state that the requirements are not one-size-fits-all.
- Paragraph 2: give two or three specific examples of how the rules differ by state. (Use real specifics — "California gives landlords 21 days, Texas gives 30 days, New York gives 'a reasonable time.'")
- Paragraph 3: explain why this is the case for using your service vs. a template. End with the second inline CTA:

[**Start your [X] — customized for your state →**](https://[your-site]/signup)]

---

## Frequently Asked Questions

[Six to eight FAQs. Each is a real question someone might type into Google. Each answer is two to four sentences. The bold lead-in is the question; the answer is plain prose.

### Is a [X] legally binding?
[2–4 sentence answer.]

### Can I write my own [X]?
[2–4 sentence answer. The answer should acknowledge the DIY option while naming why pro is better.]

### How long does it take?
[2–4 sentence answer with specific time estimates for both traditional and the user's product.]

### What if [the failure case]?
[2–4 sentence answer that names the escalation options.]

### How much does [X] cost?
[Restate the comparison from above in one paragraph.]

### Can [X] be done via [alternative channel]?
[2–4 sentence answer.]

### Do I need [X] specialist for this?
[2–4 sentence answer that positions your service as the middle path.]

The FAQ section earns its own H2 slot for SEO reasons (FAQ schema is a featured snippet vehicle) AND it is the section the impatient reader scrolls to. Make these answers stand alone.]

---

## Get Your [X] Today

[Final CTA section. Four or five short paragraphs.

- Paragraph 1: one-sentence value proposition.
- Paragraph 2: one-sentence summary of what's distinctive — specific savings percentage, specific volume done ("Over 1,000 letters delivered across all 50 states"), specific quality marker.
- Paragraph 3: a "Three simple steps" numbered list (1. Submit, 2. Review, 3. Delivered).
- Paragraph 4: one-line objection-handling ("No office visits. No hourly billing. No waiting weeks for a callback.")

End with the third inline CTA:

[**Get your [X] now →**](https://[your-site]/signup)]
```

### Compositional notes specific to this format

- **The opening must use the canon craft** even though the format is conventional. Specific scenarios beat generic claims. Three short sentences ("Someone owes you money. A contractor walked away. Your landlord is sitting on your security deposit.") is a much stronger opening than a paragraph of throat-clearing
- **Bold lead-ins are the rhythm of the format.** Each section uses bold-led paragraphs (a noun phrase followed by a period, then the prose explanation). This is the readable equivalent of bullets without losing the prose feel
- **CTAs are inline, not stacked at the end.** Three CTAs: one after pricing, one after state considerations, one in the final section. Each one appears at the moment the reader is most likely to convert
- **Specifics carry the credibility.** Real statistics. Real prices. Real state-by-state differences. Generic claims ("studies show," "experts agree") destroy trust in this format
- **Length discipline.** 2,500–4,500 words is the working range. Below 2,500 the pillar feels thin; above 4,500 the reader drops. Every section should have at least three paragraphs; no section should run more than eight
- **The disclaimer is mandatory** for any regulated category (legal, medical, financial, tax). Add it even when not asked

### Voice options inside this format

The canon voices that work best inside the pillar template:

- **Levine** for any finance, accounting, business-mechanics pillar. The parenthetical-as-argument works inside the bold-led structure
- **Orwell** for any pillar where plain explanation is the rhetorical position (legal, regulatory, technical). The shorter the words, the higher the trust
- **Coates's accumulation** for any pillar where the case has to be built brick-by-brick (insurance, healthcare, complex services)
- **Hitchens** only when the pillar is contrarian-by-design (a comparison piece arguing that the standard recommendation is wrong)

The canon voices that fight this format: Thompson (the gonzo style breaks reader trust in a service-page context), Wolfe (the maximalism doesn't fit), Junod (the under-modification reads as evasive in a sales-adjacent context).

## Template 2 — The opinion column / op-ed (newspaper, magazine)

**Use when:** the user asks for a column, op-ed, opinion piece. Typical length 700–1,200 words. Typical purpose: state and defend a thesis on a current event.

```
[TITLE — 6–10 words, contains the thesis or a sharp version of it]

[OPENING — 1 to 3 sentences, one of the seven canon opening moves (techniques.md §1).
News-pegged where possible. Specific where always.]

[BEAT 1 — set up the argument. One paragraph.]

[BEAT 2 — the central claim, with the strongest evidence. One or two paragraphs.]

[BEAT 3 — answer the smart-reader pushback. One paragraph.]

[BEAT 4 — (optional) the larger implication or the case that follows.]

[KICKER — one of the four canon kicker shapes (techniques.md §5). One paragraph.]
```

No bullets. No headers. Prose all the way through. The op-ed is the format that most rewards canon craft and most punishes filler.

## Template 3 — The Substack / founder essay

**Use when:** the user asks for a Substack post, founder essay, long-form personal piece. Typical length 1,200–3,500 words. Typical purpose: build a reader relationship while making a substantive point.

```
[TITLE — can be longer than an op-ed title; can be a question or a provocation]

[Optional: a one-line subtitle that frames the piece. Substack convention.]

[OPENING — 2 to 4 paragraphs.
Often a specific scene or moment from the writer's life or work.
The personal anchor that the rest of the piece is going to build out from.
Establishes voice immediately.]

[2–4 H2 sections, EACH section a beat in the argument, NOT a "point to cover."
Each section has its own internal arc — opening, development, mini-kicker.
The transitions between sections do real connective work; they're not just signposts.]

[KICKER — one paragraph, often loop-backs to the opening scene
or opens onto the next piece in a series.]
```

Substack rewards voice. Lean into the personal anchor; use the canon craft inside.

## Template 4 — The magazine feature

**Use when:** the user asks for a long-form magazine piece (Atlantic, New Yorker, Vanity Fair, magazine-style longform). Typical length 3,500–8,000+ words.

```
[TITLE — magazine titles can be poetic, ambiguous, or wry. Often paired with a deck.]

[DECK — a 1–2 sentence summary positioned below the title. Does the work the opening doesn't have to do.]

[OPENING — a full scene. 200–500 words. Cinematic. The reader is placed.
The thesis is implicit; do not state it yet.]

[3–8 sections. Each section is itself a small essay.
Sections are often unmarked except by a single section break (asterism, horizontal rule).
Some sections are scene; some are essayistic exposition; some are interviews/quotation.
The braid is the structure. The thesis emerges from the braid.]

[KICKER — often the reader's earlier scene, returned to, now meaning something else.
Or an image. Magazine kickers tend to refuse summary entirely; they trust the piece to have done its work.]
```

This is the most demanding format. Use only when the request explicitly calls for it.

## Template 5 — The viral social post / Twitter thread / LinkedIn post

**Use when:** the user asks for a tweet thread, LinkedIn post, short post designed to perform on a feed. Typical length 200–700 words.

```
[OPENING — one sentence under 20 words. The first six words have to earn the next six.
No throat-clearing. No "I've been thinking about..."]

[2–6 short paragraphs, each one or two sentences. White space is the design.
Each paragraph does one thing. The reader's job is to scroll one more time.]

[OPTIONAL: a numbered list embedded in the prose, if the content genuinely is enumerable.]

[KICKER — one sentence, often a reframe or implicit call to action.
The kicker has to be quotable.]
```

Viral format rewards brevity and the single specific. Cut twice as ruthlessly as you would in any other format.

## Template 6 — The internal memo / decision doc

**Use when:** the user asks for an internal memo, decision document, principal-level writeup at work. Typical length 500–2,000 words.

```
**TL;DR:** [The recommendation in one sentence. The reader who reads only this line should know what you're proposing.]

**Context** [2–4 sentences. What changed; what triggered this memo.]

**Options considered** [3 options, each with a one-paragraph description and a one-line tradeoff.]

**Recommendation** [The chosen option, defended. 2–3 paragraphs.]

**Risks** [The 2–3 most important risks, named, with a one-line mitigation each.]

**Next steps** [3–5 bullet items with owners and dates.]
```

Internal memos reward Amazon-style discipline (one-page if you can, two-page if you must, six-page if it's a real decision). The TL;DR is mandatory. The kicker is the recommendation, not a flourish.

---

## How to pick

Identify in Phase 1 (Inventory) which template applies, then apply it. If the user asks for "a piece about X," the default is Template 2 (op-ed) unless they specify otherwise.

If the user asks for something that doesn't fit any of the six, default to the closest match and note in the trace that you adapted it.

The voice and craft choices (Phases 3–8 of the main protocol) happen *inside* the chosen template. Template chooses the skeleton; the canon chooses the muscle.
