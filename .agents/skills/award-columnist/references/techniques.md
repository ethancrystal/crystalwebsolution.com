# Techniques — the compositional moves distilled from the canon

What the ten canonical writers do at the sentence, paragraph, and structural level — extracted into a working pattern library. Reach for the relevant section when you are stuck on a specific problem (opening, transition, kicker, scene).

---

## §1 — Opening moves (the seven that work)

The lede has one job: make sentence two feel inevitable. The canon uses seven recurring shapes.

**1. The flat factual disclosure with a tilt.** State a fact the reader can verify, slightly off-center. *"Last March, Microsoft did something strange. It paid roughly $650 million for Inflection AI and didn't acquire it."* (Levine voice.) The fact carries the load; the tilt carries the voice.

**2. The small scene that is the whole argument in miniature.** Two named people in a specific place doing a specific thing. The encounter contains the thesis. (Talese opens *Frank Sinatra Has a Cold* this way.) Use when the piece is about character or social dynamics.

**3. The misdirection.** Open somewhere apparently unrelated to the assignment. The reader thinks the piece is about A; it turns out to be about B. (Thompson at the Derby opens on a plane next to a stranger.) Use when the assigned subject is hollow or expected; the misdirection is itself an argument about the assignment.

**4. The single line that admits the trick of the essay.** A sentence that sounds offhand but is the thesis disguised as a remark. (Didion's *"It is easy to see the beginnings of things, and harder to see the ends."*) Use for memoir-essay, cultural diagnosis.

**5. The conceded provocation.** Acknowledge in the first paragraph that the reader will dismiss what is coming, then double down. (Hitchens on humor.) Use for contrarian pieces — the concession buys the room to argue.

**6. The named human anchored to a date and a document.** One specific person, one specific year, one specific transaction. The argument is built underneath the human's life. (Coates opens with Clyde Ross.) Use for long-form policy, moral arguments, anything historical.

**7. The composition as event.** Describe the photograph, the painting, the object itself. The thing in front of the reader is the lede. (Junod opens by describing the AP photograph as geometry.) Use when the piece is about an image, an object, or a thing the public has refused to look at.

**Lede anti-patterns — never use:** "In today's fast-paced world…", "Have you ever wondered…", a dictionary definition of the topic, a rhetorical question the reader can answer yes/no and move on, a statistic with no source, the throat-clearing announcement of what the piece will do ("In this piece I'll argue…").

## §2 — Sentence shapes (varying the cadence)

Uniform medium-length sentences are the AI cadence signature. The canon varies deliberately.

**The four-word verdict after a long passage.** A 60-word sentence that piles up evidence, then a four-word sentence that names it. *"It was already over."* (Didion does this constantly; so does Coates.) The white space around the short sentence does the work.

**The cascade.** Comma-separated phrases, each a small image, building to a verb at the end. Wolfe uses this for sensory immersion. Use when you want the reader to feel a wash of detail before the meaning lands.

**The long sentence with the verb early.** Hitchens's signature: 50 words, three subordinate clauses, and the reader never gets lost because the main verb arrived in word eight. The trick is structural — put the verb early, then let the qualifiers trail.

**The self-correcting parenthetical.** A confident sentence, then a parenthetical that doubts it. (DFW's signature.) Use when honest qualification matters more than rhetorical force.

**The single-sentence paragraph.** A one-sentence verdict, white space around it. Coates uses this as a judge's ruling at the end of a brief. Use sparingly — it loses force if overused.

**The em-dash for actual interruption, not pause.** A clause genuinely breaking from the spine of the sentence — like a thought intruding on another thought — and then resuming. Em-dashes used as commas are an AI tell; em-dashes used as interruption are voice.

**Diagnostic test.** After a draft, count sentence lengths in three consecutive paragraphs. If every sentence is 15–25 words, the cadence is dead. Insert two short sentences and one long one. Voice returns.

## §3 — Paragraph architecture

A paragraph is a unit of argument, not a unit of layout.

**The accumulation paragraph.** Each sentence adds one specific fact. The paragraph ends when the pile is heavy enough to hold up the next move. (Coates's signature.)

**The setup-and-knock-down paragraph.** First sentence states a position; remaining sentences dismantle it. Hitchens's combat structure.

**The scene paragraph.** All five senses in one paragraph, no commentary. Talese, Wolfe, Junod.

**The drift paragraph.** Starts in one register, ends in another. The genre shift is the move. (Wallace does this often — a paragraph starts as travel writing and ends as moral philosophy.) Use when you want the reader to find themselves somewhere they didn't agree to go.

**The verdict paragraph.** One sentence. White space. Used at the end of a section, not within.

**Anti-pattern: the topic-sentence-plus-three-supporting-sentences paragraph.** This is the structure five-paragraph-essay teachers taught in school. It is also the AI default. Real essayistic prose breaks it constantly.

## §4 — Transitions (earning every one)

The space between paragraphs is where AI prose betrays itself. The canon does not use transition words; the canon uses *implication*.

**Banned transition words:** *Furthermore. Moreover. Additionally. In addition. On the other hand. However.* All of these are signposts that the writer did not trust the reader to feel the connection.

**What to use instead:**

- **Repetition with variation.** Last paragraph ended on a word; new paragraph picks it up and tilts it. The transition is felt, not announced
- **The implied "because."** Two paragraphs in sequence read like cause and effect without the conjunction
- **The pivot through a specific.** New paragraph opens with a named person, a date, a number. The specificity is itself a transition — the reader trusts that you brought them somewhere worth being
- **The white space.** Sometimes the move from one beat to the next is too large for a transition. Use a section break (a single horizontal rule, an asterisk, a roman numeral). Trust the white space

**Test.** After a draft, delete every transition word. Reread. If a paragraph still connects to the one before it, leave it. If it doesn't, *the problem is the paragraph, not the missing transition word.* Rewrite the paragraph.

## §5 — Kicker shapes (the four that land)

The last paragraph is the part the reader remembers. The canon uses four shapes.

**1. The loop-back.** End where you began, but the opening image now means something different. (Didion.) The reader feels the piece click shut.

**2. The reframe.** A single sentence that reveals what the piece was actually about. Often the kicker is the moment the reader realizes the title was a feint. (Hitchens, often.)

**3. The implication.** Open onto the larger consequence. The piece ends by handing the reader the next question. *"And then, eight months later, the second deal closed."* The piece does not end; it implicates the future.

**4. The under-modified verdict.** A short, flat sentence with no rhetorical pyrotechnics. The flatness *is* the rhetoric. (Junod, Coates.) Use when the subject is heavy.

**Kicker anti-patterns — never use:**

- "In conclusion…"
- "To summarize…"
- "Ultimately…"
- A flat restatement of the thesis (the kicker has to *add* — reframe, implicate, loop)
- A call to action no one asked for ("So next time you…")
- A question to the reader that has no good answer ("What will you do?")

**Test.** Read the kicker aloud. If you would be embarrassed to say it that way at a dinner table, rewrite. The kicker should be quotable but not staged.

## §6 — The parenthetical-as-argument (Levine's move; DFW's move)

Parentheticals are the voice's hiding place.

**The Levine deadpan.** State the absurd thing flatly. Open a parenthetical. Undercut it. Close. *"The bank lost $6 billion on a single trade. (The trader's nickname was the Whale. This was the start of the bank's problem.)"* The parenthetical does what the sentence is too dignified to do.

**The DFW self-correction.** A confident claim, immediately doubted in parentheses. *"I think Z is true. (Although the reasons I think Z is true are not obviously different from the reasons people I disagree with think the opposite, which is uncomfortable.)"* The honesty of the doubt is the rhetorical move.

**When to use.** Whenever the main sentence cannot quite carry the joke, the doubt, the side remark, or the gear-shift in register. The parenthetical is the second voice running underneath the first.

**When not to use.** Three parentheticals in one paragraph is a tic, not a voice. Use deliberately. Cut the ones not doing work.

## §7 — Specifics that anchor

Vague prose is forgettable. The canon is full of specifics that should not, by ordinary writing-advice logic, matter — and that turn out to carry the piece.

**Named brands.** Lexington Avenue. The Hotel Pierre. A snap-brim hat. The TWA flight. (Didion, Talese.) Specificity creates the inhabited world.

**Specific dollar figures.** *$650 million*, not "hundreds of millions." *$5,000 invoice*, not "thousands of dollars." The pretension to precision (even when approximation would be accurate) signals that the writer has done the homework.

**Specific dates.** *March 15, 2026.* Not "earlier this year." Dates make claims falsifiable, which makes them trusted.

**Specific names.** *Clyde Ross*, *Harlan Ellison*, *Junior Johnson*, *Richard Drew*. Even when the named person is unknown to the reader, the naming creates the feel of testimony.

**One specific only the writer could know.** A particular café, a stray email, a remembered smell. The piece feels inhabited because someone clearly *lived* the specifics.

**Test.** In a 700-word piece, mark every concrete specific (name, brand, address, dollar figure, date). If fewer than ten, the piece is air. Add five.

## §8 — Vocabulary (Orwell's discipline)

Orwell's instruction manual, distilled:

- Never use a long word where a short one will do
- If it is possible to cut a word out, cut it out
- Never use a passive where you can use the active
- Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent
- Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous

The fifth rule is the one most often forgotten. The first four are not absolute — they are defaults that should be broken when breaking them serves the prose. The discipline is in *knowing* when you are breaking them and why.

**Latinate-versus-Anglo-Saxon as a deliberate move.** The Latinate word sounds formal, mannered, distant. The Anglo-Saxon word sounds direct, embodied, plain. Use the Latinate to set up; use the Anglo-Saxon to knock down. *"It is widely understood that the practice is salutary."* (Latinate setup.) *"It isn't."* (Anglo-Saxon hammer.)

## §9 — The voice signature (what makes one writer feel like one person)

Every canonical voice has two or three recurring moves that, used consistently, become a fingerprint.

| Writer | Voice signatures |
|--------|------------------|
| Didion | The cool observation. The "of course" confidence. The time-loop. The named-then-forgotten detail |
| Thompson | The catalogue of grotesques. The drug-paranoid aside. The reporter-as-instrument |
| Talese | The invisible narrator. The peripheral character doing the work. The cinematic scene |
| Wolfe | Italics for sound. The cascade. The maximalist sensory paragraph |
| Hitchens | Long-sentence-verb-early. The literary reference doing argument. The mock-judicial setup |
| Wallace | Footnotes that argue. The stacked parenthetical. Vocabulary register-mixing |
| Coates | The named human in every section. The widening historical circle. The single-sentence verdict |
| Levine | The deadpan-plus-parenthetical. Recurring catchphrases. Plain English for technical content |
| Orwell | Anglo-Saxon defaults. Exhibits with citations. Earned rules at the end |
| Junod | Under-modified adjectives. Sentences that breathe. Refusal of consolation |

When writing in a chosen voice, pick *two or three* of that writer's signatures and use them consistently. Trying to deploy all of them at once produces pastiche. Two used reliably produces voice.

## §10 — The fingerprint test

After completing a draft, take three sentences at random — one from the opening, one from the middle, one from the kicker. Read them in isolation.

Three questions:
- Could these sentences have been written by any of fifty other writers in this genre?
- Do they share a fingerprint with each other, or do they read like three different essays?
- Would a reader who has read three of the writer's previous pieces recognize this as the same writer?

If the answer to any of the three is unsatisfying, the composition pass (Phase 8) has not done its job. Go back. The fingerprint test is the one diagnostic that matters most, because *voice is what the reader notices first, what the reader remembers longest, and what AI prose most consistently lacks*.
