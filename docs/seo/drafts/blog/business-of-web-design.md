A business decides its website needs to change. The homepage does not reflect the brand. The navigation was added to over three years by three different people. The contact form works, but nobody fills it out because the page around it gives no reason to trust the business enough to send a message.

At that point, someone in the room says what everyone is thinking: "we need a new website."

And someone else says what nobody wants to admit: "we do not know what we are actually buying."

This is the business of web design. Not the software. Not the color palette. The decisions a visitor makes — before they ever fill out a form, before they call, before they leave — are shaped by what the design does, and most of those decisions are made in the first five seconds.

## What web design actually is

Web design is the discipline of shaping what a visitor understands and does on a way. It covers information architecture (what is on the page and why it is in that order), visual design (type, color, imagery, spacing), interaction design (what moves, what confirms, what is removed), and the bridge between design and engineering (what gets built, what gets handed off, what the team on your side actually receives at the end).

It is not a homepage.

A homepage is one output. Web design is the system that makes the homepage, the services page, the case study, the contact form, and the forty-four page read as one coherent thing — on a laptop, on a phone, and in the head of the person who lands on any of them without knowing who you are.

## What it is not

**It is not software.** Web design does not include the build — the engineering, the hosting, the database, the CMS. Some studios design and build under one roof. Some hand the design off to a separate engineering team. The question is not which is better; the question is whether you know which one you are buying.

**It is not branding.** A new logo, a new palette, a new type system — that is brand identity work. It often runs alongside a redesign, and sometimes a redesign uncovers the fact that the brand itself needs work first. But a design RFP that assumes a finished brand and one that is inventing one are two different projects, and the price changes accordingly.

**It is not maintenance.** Design ends. Maintenance — content updates, new pages, small iterations — is an ongoing relationship or an internal capability, not the engagement that produced the design.

## What a design engagement looks like

Every studio's process has its own shape, but a serious one moves through the same phases:

1. **Discovery** — goals, audience, the one job the site must do, what exists now, what must survive the redesign, what can be left behind. The studio reads your current analytics, your current site, your current brand assets, and asks questions nobody in your organization has asked out loud yet.

2. **Structure** — information architecture, user flows, key page templates. The shape of the thing before it has a color on it. This is where you find out whether the current navigation made sense, or whether it was just the order things were added.

3. **Design** — visual system, layout, motion direction, the type scale and spacing that makes the site read as one thing. Some studios work in high-fidelity screen mockups; others design directly in the browser. Both are legitimate — the question is whether you can see it working on the devices your visitors actually use.

4. **Build handoff** — the finished design is the same file as the shipped page, or a close enough approximation that your engineering team knows exactly what to build. A design that only exists as a file on a laptop is a promise, not a product.

5. **Refine** — measured against real behavior, not opinions. The shipped page gets watched. The parts that do not hold up get adjusted. The parts that do, stay.

## What the studio needs from you

A studio can only design well if it knows what it is designing *for*. The brief you give it determines how close the first round is to what you actually want.

**Outcomes, not moods.** "Make it look modern" is not a brief. "A first-time visitor should understand what we sell and what to do next in under ten seconds" is a brief. One tells the studio what feeling to chase; the other tells it what job the page must do, and lets it figure out the feeling that gets there.

**What exists now.** Current site, current platform, current analytics, current brand assets. The studio needs to know what it is working with and what it is working around — integrations that must survive, content that must migrate, logins that must keep working.

**What is in scope, and what is not.** Development, copywriting, photography, branding, ongoing maintenance, SEO, paid media — each is a separate discipline. If you are unsure whether something is included, say so. A studio will either quietly include it and price it, or quietly exclude it and you will find out later.

**The real constraints.** Launch dates that have a reason behind them. A review window on your side — a design cycle that needs feedback within two business days cannot run on a review window of two weeks. The stakeholders whose opinions matter and the ones whose preferences are just noise.

**A budget range, not a secret.** Studios price to the budget. Withhold it, and you get proposals spread across an order of magnitude, most of them for a project you would never buy. A range produces proposals you can actually compare, and it screens out mismatches before anyone spends a day designing.

## What it costs

Scope varies too much for a public price to mean anything. A custom web design engagement for a business with a real audience and real deliverables is not a commodity.

What drives the cost: the number of unique page templates, the complexity of the interaction and motion, the depth of the discovery, the number of revision rounds, whether the brand identity exists or needs to be created, and whether the studio is also building what it designs.

What does not drive the cost: the number of pages that reuse the same template. Adding a new case study to an existing template is not the same effort as designing the template in the first place.

## How to tell the difference

A studio that leads with its portfolio is showing you what it has done. A studio that leads with questions about your business is showing you how it thinks.

The second kind is rarer and more valuable. The portfolio tells you it can produce beautiful work. The questions tell you it will understand your specific situation before it starts designing for it — because a design that is beautiful and wrong for your audience is more expensive than no design at all. You will pay for it twice: once for the engagement, and again when you rebuild the result.

Before you hire anyone, ask what happens after the design is handed off. A studio that delivers a picture of what the site should look like has delivered a promise. A studio that delivers a working component library, a documented type scale, and a handoff the engineering team can actually build from has delivered something real.
