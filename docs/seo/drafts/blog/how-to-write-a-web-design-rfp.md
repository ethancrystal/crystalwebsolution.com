A web design RFP asks a studio to commit to a visual direction, a scope, and a price before either side has seen the work. The better the brief, the less the proposal is a guess — and the more it is a real answer.

This guide is written from the receiving end: what makes the difference between a design RFP a studio can respond to properly and one where every bidder is designing to a fog. It includes a template you can copy and a scorecard for comparing what comes back.

It sits alongside the [web development RFP guide](/blog/web-development-rfp-guide), which covers the same idea for the build side. Read both if your project includes a redesign plus a rebuild — the design and the engineering have different questions worth answering up front.

## What a web design RFP is actually for

A request for proposal does three jobs at once. It tells studios whether the project is a fit, so the wrong ones can bow out early. It gives the right ones enough to estimate visual scope, production effort, and risk without a week of discovery calls. And it forces *you* to make decisions before you commit money — which is where most of the value is, whether or not you end up sending it.

That last point matters. If you cannot answer a question in the template below, that is not a gap in the document. It is a decision you have not made yet, and a studio's proposal will make it for you — usually in whichever direction is safest for them to sell.

## The nine things every web design RFP needs

### 1. Who you are, in one paragraph

Not your history. What you sell, to whom, and what the site has to do for that. "We are a regional furniture maker; the site's job is to make people trust us enough to place a wholesale order" tells a studio more than three pages of company background.

### 2. Why now

Something prompted this. The current site cannot be updated, the brand has outgrown its look, a competitor just raised the bar, a rebrand has landed, the business has a new audience. Name it. The trigger tells the studio what "better" means to you and what they must not break along the way.

### 3. What the design must do — as outcomes, not visuals

Write the outcomes first: "a first-time visitor should understand what we sell and what to do next in under ten seconds." Then, separately, list any visual references you already have. Outcomes let a studio propose a better route than the one you imagined. A mood-board-only brief gets you exactly the look you pointed at, which is rarely the site you wanted.

### 4. What exists today

Current site, its platform, any brand assets already owned (logo, colors, type, photography, copy), and what must carry over. If the brand itself needs work, say whether that is a separate engagement or part of this one — a studio pricing a design RFP needs to know whether it is designing from a finished identity or inventing one.

### 5. Scope boundaries

State what is in and, just as importantly, what is out. Development, content, photography, branding, ongoing maintenance, SEO, paid ads — each is a separate discipline, and a studio will either quietly include it (and price it) or quietly exclude it (and you will find out later). Decide, and write it down. If you are unsure, say "please quote these as optional line items."

### 6. Brand state — finished or in-flight

Be explicit. If you already have a logo, palette, and type system, attach them. If you have a brand guide, share it. If the brand is being redesigned at the same time, say so, and say who owns that work — because a design RFP that assumes a stable brand and one that is inventing one are two different projects.

### 7. Budget — a range, not a secret

Studios price to the budget. If you withhold it, you get proposals spread across an order of magnitude, most of them for a project you would never buy. A range ("we expect this to land between X and Y; tell us what changes at each end") produces proposals you can actually compare, and it screens out mismatches before anyone spends a day designing.

### 8. Timeline, with the real constraints

A launch date is only useful with the reason behind it: a season, a trade show, a campaign, a rebrand that must go live together. Say which dates are fixed and which are preferences. And be honest about your own availability — a design cycle that needs your feedback within two business days cannot run on a review window of two weeks.

### 9. How you will decide

List your evaluation criteria and their weights before you read a single proposal. This is the section design RFPs most often leave out, and it is the one that stops the decision being made on gut feel and the prettiest deck. A simple version is in the scorecard below.

## What to leave out

**Boilerplate legal terms.** Put them in the contract, not the RFP. A twelve-page terms appendix signals a procurement process that will be as heavy as the document, and good small studios will pass.

**A page-by-page wireframe you have not tested.** Share the pages you know you need; let the studio propose structure. If you have already decided the entire architecture, you are hiring a builder, not a design partner — which is fine, but say that instead.

**Speculative redesigns.** Asking a studio to redesign your current homepage as part of the proposal means the ones who take it seriously do unpaid work, and the ones who do not send stock decks. Ask for relevant past work and a written approach instead. If you want to see how a studio thinks, pay for a short paid discovery engagement with your top two.

**Every stakeholder's taste list.** Consolidate before you send. Contradictory visual wishes from different departments are the first thing an experienced studio notices, and they price the friction in.

## A template you can copy

Replace everything in brackets. Delete sections that do not apply rather than leaving them vague. The template splits the nine items above into ten sections — it adds a list of what you want *in* the proposals — because that is the part studios most often have to ask about.

```
WEB DESIGN RFP — [Company name]
Issued: [date]   Proposals due: [date]   Decision by: [date]   Contact: [name, email]

1. ABOUT US
[One paragraph: what you do, for whom, and what the website has to accomplish.]

2. WHY THIS PROJECT, WHY NOW
[The trigger. What is broken, changing, or being launched.]

3. DESIRED OUTCOMES
The new design must make it possible for:
- [a visitor type] to [understand / do a thing] [measure of ease or speed]
- [a visitor type] to [feel / trust / act] in a specific way
- [our team] to [update / manage / hand off] without [constraint]

4. BRAND STATE
- Logo: [final / in progress / not started]
- Palette and type: [defined / open to recommendation]
- Photography and brand imagery: [owned / needs sourcing / open]
- Brand guide: [exists and attached / exists and will share on request / not yet]

5. KNOWN REQUIREMENTS
- Must integrate with: [CRM / payments / booking / other — name the products, if any]
- Must support: [languages, accessibility standard, regions, devices]
- Must migrate: [approx. page count, media, user accounts, content]
- Must not change: [anything that has to survive the redesign]
- Accessibility: [the standard and level you need to meet, if any]
- Performance: [what "fast enough" means for your audience and devices]
- Browsers and devices: [what must be supported; what can be dropped]

6. SCOPE
In scope: [design, prototyping, revisions, developer handoff, style guide]
Out of scope: [development, copywriting, photography, branding, ongoing SEO, paid media]
Please quote as optional line items: [development, content, branding, maintenance, other]

7. BUDGET
We expect the core project to fall between [low] and [high]. Please tell us
what you would recommend at each end of that range and what changes between them.

8. TIMELINE
Target launch: [date] — [fixed / preferred] because [reason].
Our side can review and approve work within [N] business days.

9. WHAT TO INCLUDE IN YOUR PROPOSAL
- Your understanding of the project and your proposed visual direction
- Two or three relevant examples of past work, with what you were responsible for
- Team: who would design this and their roles
- Process: discovery, concepts, revision rounds, approval points, handoff
- Pricing: what is included, what is optional, payment schedule
- Assumptions and exclusions
- What happens after handoff: style guide, assets, documentation, support

10. PROCESS
Questions accepted until [date]; answers shared with all bidders.
Shortlisted studios will be invited to a [30/60]-minute call the week of [date].
```

## Scoring the proposals you get back

Weight these before proposals arrive, then score each one on the same sheet. Adjust the weights to your situation; the point is that they are written down first.

1. **Understanding of the problem (25%)** — Did they restate your outcomes in their own words, and did they get it right? A proposal that leads with a portfolio piece instead of your problem has not read the RFP.
2. **Relevance of past work (20%)** — Similar audience, similar industry, or similar scale. Prettiness is not relevance.
3. **Process and who does the work (20%)** — Is there a named designer who will actually do this? Do they explain *how*, not just *that*?
4. **Fit with your brand state (15%)** — Did they price the right project? A studio that assumed a finished brand when yours is in flux priced the wrong thing.
5. **Price and value (10%)** — Within range, with clear assumptions. The cheapest proposal is only a bargain if its exclusions do not contain the expensive parts.
6. **After handoff (10%)** — Style guide, asset delivery, documentation, who owns what, what support costs.

A proposal that scores well everywhere but leaves you unable to answer "what do we actually receive at the end?" is not finished. Ask.

## Questions to ask on the call

- Walk me through how you would approach [your hardest constraint].
- What in this RFP worries you?
- What would you cut if the budget came in at the low end?
- Who is my day-to-day contact, and what is their capacity right now?
- Show me a project where the design had to change after it was built, and what you changed afterwards.

The second question is the useful one. A studio that says "nothing" has either not read it closely or is not going to tell you when something goes wrong later.

## Before you send it

- Every outcome in section 3 has a way to measure it
- Brand state is stated honestly — finished, in-flight, or not started
- Budget is a range with a reason for the ends
- Out-of-scope list exists and someone senior has signed off on it
- Evaluation weights are written down and shared internally
- One named contact who can answer questions within two business days
- The deadline gives studios at least two weeks — good ones are busy

If the document takes you a full day to write, that is a day well spent. The decisions you make in it are the ones that would otherwise be made for you, at your expense, halfway through the design.
