# Exemplar Pastiches

These are original Claude-written prose passages demonstrating the cadence and structural moves of each writer in the voice library. They are **not quotations** and do not represent the views of the writers named. They are craft exercises — pastiches, in the same sense that an art student might paint a study after Vermeer to learn his brushwork.

All passages are written on the same topic — **side projects** — so the cadence differences between voices are visible at a glance. Read them in sequence: the same idea sounds entirely different in each voice, and that difference is the thing being studied.

When picking a voice in Phase 3, scan the relevant pastiche to absorb the rhythm. Don't copy phrases — internalize the *structural moves*. The pastiche is a tuning fork, not a template.

---

## Paul Graham

Most side projects fail because they're not really projects. They're hobbies dressed up. A project has a thing you're trying to do; a hobby has a thing you're trying to feel. The test is simple. If you stopped enjoying it tomorrow, would you keep going? If yes, you have a project. If no, you have a hobby, and that's fine — but stop telling people you're building a startup. The confusion is expensive. People with hobbies make career choices appropriate for people with projects, and people with projects undersell themselves because they think they're just messing around. [1]

[1] The asymmetry only goes one way. A hobby can grow into a project, but a project can't shrink into a hobby without losing what made it a project.

## Tim Urban

OK so here's the weird thing about side projects.

You start one. You're psyched. Week 1, you're a god — you've designed the schema, you've written a manifesto, you've registered a domain (you bought TWO domains, just in case). Week 2, the universe gives you a little nudge. Maybe your day job gets busy. Maybe your dog gets sick. Maybe you remember you were supposed to have a personality. By Week 3, the project has joined what I call the Side Project Graveyard, which (if you're like me) now contains roughly 47 corpses, all of which felt INEVITABLE at the moment of conception.

The really annoying part? The next idea you have feels just as exciting. Your brain doesn't learn. Your brain *cannot* learn. Your brain is, in this respect, a goldfish in a Cybertruck.

## Morgan Housel

Most side projects don't fail.

They get postponed.

Postponed long enough, postponement becomes failure. But the failure is invisible. There's no funeral, no obituary, no last commit. Just a folder somewhere getting older.

The people who finish their side projects aren't more disciplined than the people who don't. They made the project smaller.

A 100-hour project finishes. A 10,000-hour project never starts.

The cost of finishing is always lower than the cost of imagining.

## Ben Thompson

The economics of side projects have inverted over the past decade, and the inversion has been clean enough that the implications are still being worked out in real time. Distribution, once the binding constraint, is now effectively free; what's scarce is the founder's attention. The consequence is striking. What used to be the build phase — months of engineering followed by a launch event — has compressed into a weekend. What used to be the launch event — one tweet, one Show HN post, one press release — has stretched into a years-long compounding loop in which the project must be marketed continuously to retain any of the audience it briefly acquires. The side project, in other words, has become less like product development and more like content marketing. And content marketing, for all its surface appeal, has a known failure mode: the marketer becomes the product, and the product becomes a flag the marketer plants. This is fine for personal brand building. It is fatal for actually shipping software.

## David Brooks

I had coffee last month with a thirty-two-year-old engineer in Austin who was on his fourth side project of the year. He spoke about it the way an earlier generation spoke about a starter home: as a hedge against precarity, a foundation, a thing he was building outside the institutions that had failed to promise him a future. He was not bitter about it. He was almost cheerful.

I think we are living through a slow unbundling of work, in which the side project has become a kind of secular vocation. For the people I've been talking to — and there are a lot of them, in their late twenties and early thirties, scattered across cities I would not have predicted — the side project is the place where they are *most themselves*. The day job pays. The side project means.

This is not, on balance, a happy development. A society in which meaning has retreated from the institutions that used to provide it, and reassembled in the off-hours of its most talented young people, is a society that has lost something.

## Ezra Klein

There's a common defense of the side project that I want to take seriously before I disagree with it.

The argument goes like this: even if 95% of side projects fail, the optionality is the point. You can't predict which idea will hit, and the cost of trying any given one is low. So you keep trying things. You stay in motion. You expose yourself to luck.

I find this convincing, up to a point. The point is roughly the moment when "trying things" becomes a substitute for finishing anything. And here is where I think the maximalist case for optionality breaks down. Optionality has a cost that the standard defense doesn't reckon with: every started-and-abandoned project teaches your brain that starting is the satisfying part. You get the dopamine of the new idea, the manifesto, the domain name. You don't get the harder, less rewarding dopamine of shipping the thing.

What I keep coming back to is that the rationalist defense of side projects assumes a kind of self-discipline most people don't actually have. The case is right in principle and wrong in practice — and the gap between those two is where most careers get stuck.

## Christopher Hitchens

There is, in the late-imperial Silicon Valley imagination, a category of person known as the "serial side-project guy." He is the heir, whether he knows it or not, to a long and somewhat undistinguished tradition: the dilettante, the man of many enthusiasms and few accomplishments, whom Cyril Connolly diagnosed two generations ago in *Enemies of Promise* and whom Auden had in mind, I think, when he wrote about the man who was always *about* to do something. The difference is that the modern version has been told he is admirable. He mistakes his condition for an asset; he markets it under the name "optionality"; he is, against all aesthetic and historical evidence, rewarded for it. One reads his LinkedIn — for there is no escaping his LinkedIn — and finds the wreckage of a serious life dressed up as a strategy. It is a sad business, made sadder by the fact that he himself is the last to see it.

## Matt Levine

The economics of side projects are pretty funny. You spend your weekend building software that you intend to sell for $9 a month to people who, mathematically, do not yet exist (and may, in the relevant timeframe, never exist — but anyway). You do this instead of, say, sleeping, or having a personality. The dollar-per-hour math is grim (if you are charitable about what counts as an hour) and grim-squared (if you are not).

And yet — and this is the part finance never quite explains — you keep doing it. You will keep doing it. Some of you are doing it right now, on a second monitor, while reading this. (Hi.) Because at some level you're not optimizing for dollars at all. You're paying yourself in the option to be a different kind of person. That's a real return. It just doesn't show up on the K-1. The IRS is, in this one specific respect, behind the curve, which is unusual for the IRS but consistent with their general posture toward the digital economy.

## Naval

A side project is a bet on yourself.

The bet is small. The asymmetry is large.

Most side projects fail. The ones that don't change everything.

You aren't paid for the side project. You're paid in compounding optionality.

The person who finishes ten small projects is more dangerous than the person who plans one large one.

Specific knowledge is built in side projects, not jobs.

The world does not need your side project. You need your side project.

Build anyway.

## Anne Helen Petersen (cultural-essay mode)

I keep thinking about the side project — not as a thing, but as a category of *feeling*.

We didn't always have side projects. We had jobs, and we had hobbies, and the line between them was clear and largely uncontested: the job fed you, the hobby didn't, and the difference was the difference between obligation and pleasure. My grandfather built model trains in the basement. He did not refer to them as "passive income," because passive income was not, at that point, a concept that haunted middle-class life.

The side project erases the line between obligation and pleasure, and in erasing it, asks something of us that previous generations weren't expected to give: a portion of our leisure converted, hopefully but not certainly, into income. It's not just a way of making things. It's a way of conscripting your evenings into the labor market.

What we talk about when we talk about side projects, in other words, is what we no longer believe our day jobs can provide — meaning, stability, the possibility of more — and what we've quietly accepted we'll have to assemble ourselves, on weekends, in the time that used to be ours.

That engineer in the basement, the one with the trains, was building something he didn't expect anyone to buy. We used to call that a hobby. Some of us would like that word back.

## Reading these in sequence

Notice:
- **How the same observation lands differently in each voice.** Graham's *project vs. hobby* test, Housel's *postponement becomes failure*, Klein's *trying things becomes a substitute*, Petersen's *the line between obligation and pleasure* — these are kin observations dressed in different cadences.
- **What each voice can and cannot do.** Naval's compression is powerful for declaration but couldn't sustain a 1500-word argument. Brooks's scenic warmth can carry a long piece but can't do raw analytic compression. Pick voices for what they're built to do, not just for vibes.
- **The structural moves are more transferable than the lexical tells.** "And yet" used in a non-PG voice will read like a costume; the structural move (mark a logical pivot with a short phrase) is the actually useful thing to steal.

When you write a real piece, the goal is not to imitate any of these. It's to absorb the *moves* and deploy them in service of the user's actual argument and voice.
